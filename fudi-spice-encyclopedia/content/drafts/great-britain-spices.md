Britain's spice story has two chapters, and both are about warmth. The signature spices are curry powder, allspice, clove and cinnamon, with mustard, cayenne, nutmeg, mace, ginger and juniper in support.

## The empire's legacy

One of the culinary legacies of the British Empire is a taste for South Asian spicing, blended into all-purpose curry powders. Curry itself is Britain's unofficial "national dish", and curry powder turns up in the gravy-like sauce served with chips, in the vegetable preserve piccalilli, and in the summer buffet staple Coronation chicken. Curry powder is a mix that includes fenugreek, turmeric, ginger and cumin.

## The festive spices

Further back, Britain's medieval past gave it a love of warming, aromatic spices in sweet bakes and drinks for the cold months. Cloves, cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger and mace are immediately evocative of the festive season to any Brit. Saffron and white pepper were once much more popular, and now survive mostly in Cornish buns and preserved meats. Coriander seeds still flavour Lancashire's Goosnargh cakes.

## Mulling spice

Combine 2 cinnamon sticks, 6 cloves, 6 allspice berries, half a nutmeg and 2 bay leaves, and add them to a pan of red wine or cider. Heat to simmering point (or tie the spices in muslin to retrieve them easily), then add sugar or honey, slices of orange or lemon, and rum or sloe gin to taste. This is enough for two bottles of red wine or 1.75 litres of still cider, traditionally drunk at winter celebrations from Bonfire Night to Twelfth Night orchard wassails.
