France doesn't immediately spring to mind as a spice-rich cuisine, and yet the very word "spice" comes from the Old French *espice*, meaning "seasoning". The signature spices are garlic, mustard, fennel and nutmeg, with caraway, vanilla, anise, white pepper, juniper and saffron in support.

## Pierre Poivre

In the 18th century the French botanist Pierre Poivre broke the Dutch stranglehold on cloves and nutmeg through smuggling raids on the Moluccas, the Spice Islands. We come back to that story in Southeast Asia.

## Small spices, big flavours

It's hard to imagine a classic Dauphinoise potato without nutmeg, Provençal cookery without the aniseed notes of fennel, or French cooking in general without garlic or Dijon and Bordeaux mustards. Heat comes from garlic and pepper rather than chilli, though chilli does feature in Basque and Provençal dishes, and the mild Espelette chilli spices traditional French Basque cooking. Saffron flavours the southern fish stew bouillabaisse, and the French kitchen also uses unusual spices like angelica stalk in sweets and digestif liqueurs, and tonka bean in creams and custards.

## Quatre épices

Grind 1 tablespoon of white peppercorns and 1 teaspoon of cloves. Combine with 1 teaspoon of ground nutmeg and 1 teaspoon of ground ginger. This "four spices" mix seasons terrines. Proportions can be tweaked, and, confusingly, cinnamon is sometimes added as a fifth spice. For a sweet-bake version, replace the white pepper with allspice or cinnamon.
