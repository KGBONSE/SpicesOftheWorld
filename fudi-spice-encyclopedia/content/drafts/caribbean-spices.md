When European explorers went looking for the Indies, they found the Caribbean instead. What followed was centuries of empire-building that nearly wiped out the islands' indigenous peoples, and turned the West Indies into a new hub of the spice trade. It is a painful beginning, and it is the reason the food here is so extraordinary.

## Adventurous, bold, spicy

The signature spices are Scotch bonnet chilli, allspice, clove, ginger, nutmeg and cinnamon, with black pepper, cumin, garlic, paprika and habanero in support. Behind them come turmeric, coriander, fenugreek, cayenne and nigella.

## Everyone is in the pot

Caribbean cooking brings together Amerindian, African, Creole, Spanish, Dutch, Portuguese, British, Latin American, Persian, South Asian and Indonesian influences, all in one place. You can taste it in small details. Turmeric, coriander and fenugreek sit at the heart of West Indian curry powder, a direct import from South Asia. Roasted nigella seeds flavour breads and vegetable dishes. Mexican oregano, with its citrus and aniseed notes, often turns up in chilli powder blends.

## A thread back to Africa

Here is a link worth following. Calabash nutmeg crossed the Atlantic through the European slave trade and became known as Jamaican nutmeg, which we tell in the Central Africa story. The Caribbean spice rack has roots on both sides of the ocean.

## A Jamaican jerk rub

Jerk was invented in Jamaica and adapted across the islands, and it has its own story on this site. Grind 2 teaspoons of allspice, 1 teaspoon of whole black peppercorns and half a teaspoon of cloves. Combine with 1 tablespoon of chipotle or cayenne powder, 1 teaspoon of paprika, 1 teaspoon of grated nutmeg, 1 teaspoon of ground ginger, half a teaspoon of cinnamon, 2 teaspoons each of onion powder and garlic powder, 1 teaspoon of dried thyme, 2 tablespoons of brown sugar and 2 teaspoons of sea salt. Rub it onto chicken, fish, pork or beef. The longer it marinates, the more intense the flavour.
