Lebanese mezze may be the most famous thing the Middle East has ever shared with the rest of the world: small plates, one after another, spread across a table until you have lost count. Behind them sits a cook who uses spice generously and with a lot of love.

## The great hummus debate

Few dishes start an argument like hummus. Lebanese and Israeli cooks both love it fiercely, both say it is theirs, and where it truly began is hotly disputed. It reminds me of the way Ghana, Nigeria and, to some extent, Senegal go back and forth over jollof rice. I won't pick a side. The only fair answer is to eat it at both tables, and to notice that a dish worth fighting over is a dish worth loving.

## The seven-spice blend

The Lebanese baharat is made from seven spices: coriander, cinnamon, black pepper, allspice, clove, ginger and nutmeg. It is a kitchen staple, rubbed into meats, stirred into soups and stews, and used to season mezze. Around it you will find garlic, sumac, paprika, cumin, cardamom, sesame and fenugreek, with saffron, turmeric, caraway and anise on the supplementary shelf.

## A sweet tooth with aniseed

Lebanese desserts are a joy. Aniseed is the favourite flavouring, in meghli, a spiced rice pudding, and in sfouf, a tea cake. If you have only ever met aniseed in sweets, try it in a warm, soft cake.

## Taklia

Taklia is a small finishing touch that changes a whole pot. Gently fry 3 sliced garlic cloves in 2 tablespoons of olive oil until fragrant, but don't let them colour. Pound them with 1 teaspoon of ground coriander, a pinch of cayenne and half a teaspoon of sea salt. Add it to soups and stews just before serving.
