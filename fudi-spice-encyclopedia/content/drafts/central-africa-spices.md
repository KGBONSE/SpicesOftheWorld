There is something quietly moving about the spice cupboard of Central Africa: it barely changed. While so much of the world's cooking was reshaped by trade, the kitchens of Cameroon, the south of Nigeria, Angola and the Democratic Republic of the Congo held on to their own, to seeds and barks and pods that grow close by, that taste of nothing else, and that most of us outside the region have never even met.

## Nutty, pungent, local

Three spices carry the region. Bobimbi, the bark and seeds that do the work other kitchens give to garlic. Njangsa, the seeds that bring a nutty richness. And mbongo, dark and pungent, with a slow heat that lingers. Around them sit calabash nutmeg, ginger, grains of paradise and the Scotch bonnet chilli.

That last one is the newcomer. Chillies only arrived with Portuguese explorers in the fifteenth century, and apart from them, Central African cooking stayed remarkably faithful to what already grew there. So where did the heat come from before? From three native spices: Ashanti pepper, grains of Selim and mbongo, each one giving a lingering warmth with a faint lift of citrus at the end.

## One name, four spices

Here is the part that trips everyone up. Across the region, "grains of paradise" is a name used for four different spices: Ashanti pepper, mbongo, grains of Selim, and the true grains of paradise themselves. So when a cook tells you there are grains of paradise in the pot, it is worth asking which one they mean. Names travel more easily than spices do, and these ones have wandered a long way.

## A nutmeg that crossed an ocean

Calabash nutmeg has a harder story. It was carried to the Caribbean through the European slave trade, and over time it became known there as Jamaican nutmeg. It is worth sitting with that for a moment: a spice you might think of as Caribbean arrived there with people taken from this part of Africa. The Caribbean kitchen has Central African roots in its cupboard, whether or not the name on the jar remembers it.

## A mix to try at home

This is a simple mbongo mix, the kind of thing used to flavour Cameroon's black stew, mbongo tchobi. Grind together:

- whole mbongo (or black cardamom, if you can't find it)
- bobimbi, or wild garlic or chives
- njangsa seeds, or unsalted peanuts
- calabash nutmeg, or freshly grated nutmeg
- a little grains of paradise
- a few garlic cloves and some chopped onion

Start small and taste as you go. It is a mix that rewards patience, and it will teach you more about this part of the world than any list of facts could.
