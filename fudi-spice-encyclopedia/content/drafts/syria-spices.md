Syria often gets overlooked in the story of Middle Eastern food, tucked between the better-known kitchens of Turkey and Lebanon. That is a shame, because the cooking here is generous and beautiful: fresh herbs, cherries, dates, pomegranates and nuts, all laced with a spice rack that is fragrant rather than fierce.

## A milder hand

Syrian cooks tend to spice more gently than their neighbours. You can see it in small choices, like reaching for safflower where others would pay for saffron. The signature spices are Aleppo pepper, sumac, allspice, black pepper and garlic, with cumin, cinnamon, cardamom, nigella and sesame close behind.

## Aleppo pepper

If one thing stands out, it is Aleppo pepper. It is a mild chilli flake produced around the ancient Silk Road town of Aleppo, with all the fruitiness of a chilli and very little of the burn. A pinch on eggs, yoghurt or roasted vegetables is one of the quiet pleasures of this part of the world.

## One word, many blends

Across the Middle East you will hear the word baharat. It simply means "spices", and it is the blend that defines the whole region, though the exact mix shifts from place to place and from household to household.

## Make your own za'atar

Dry-roast 2 tablespoons of cumin seeds with 1 teaspoon of sea salt until fragrant, then grind them. Separately, dry-roast 2 tablespoons of sesame seeds until golden. Combine everything with 2 tablespoons of dried oregano and 2 tablespoons of sumac. Scatter it over hummus, labneh, cheese, meat or fish, or stir it into olive oil and eat it with warm bread.
