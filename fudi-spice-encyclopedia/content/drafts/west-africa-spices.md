West Africa is where my family's cooking comes from, and it is hot, pungent and smoky in the very best way. Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal and the countries around them share a backbone of chilli, ginger and garlic, and then each one builds something entirely its own on top.

## The supporting cast

The star spices are the ones you would expect. Then come the ones that make the region's cooking unmistakable: grains of Selim, grains of paradise, iru (fermented locust bean seeds), prekese, nutmeg and bay. If you have ever wondered what gives a West African stew that deep, savoury, lingering taste, much of the answer is in that list.

## A region split by language

One of the most interesting things about West African food is how colonial history drew lines through it. In Francophone countries, cooking tends to favour mustard paste and souring agents like vinegar or lemon, which is the secret behind Senegal's famous poulet yassa. In Anglophone countries, flavour is built through smoking, fermenting and drying: dried and smoked crayfish, fermented locust beans, and deep, slow-cooked sauces.

## The jollof debate

And then there is jollof rice, the dish that can start an argument at any West African table. Ghana, Nigeria and, to some extent, Senegal each have their own claim and their own way of making it, and none of them will back down. I won't pick a side.

## Yaji, the street-food spice

Yaji is the mix behind suya, the street-food kebabs that fill the air with smoke after dark. Combine grains of paradise, grains of Selim, whole Ashanti pepper, crushed kuli-kuli (peanut puffs), ginger powder, cayenne flakes, a crumbled stock cube, salt and black pepper. Suya has its own story on this site.
