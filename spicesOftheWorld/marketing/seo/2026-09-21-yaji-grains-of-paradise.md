### Yaji Spice Blend: The West African Rub Behind Suya and Chinchinga

Growing up in Ghana, there was one stall at Mokola Market my mum, God bless her soul, would always stop at — before we even got to the fish, before the plantain, before the koobi, before anything else. She'd pick up a handful of small, reddish-brown seeds, crack one between her fingers, and let me smell it. Sharp, peppery, warm — nothing like the black pepper I knew from school dinners back home. That smell planted the seeds for everything that came after: my love for spices, for farming, for cooking. So today, I want to take you back to that exact stall, for the spice Europe once called "grains of paradise."

#### Where it actually comes from

Grains of paradise doesn't come from anywhere fancy — it comes from right here, West Africa. Ghana, today, is still the main place in the world growing it. There's a whole stretch of coastline that used to be called the Grain Coast, the Pepper Coast, because of how much of this spice moved through it. Its cousin, grains of selim, travels even further — from Ethiopia all the way to Ghana, and it's especially loved in southern Nigeria too.

#### A pepper substitute that outlived the substitution

Here's the thing that gets me: hundreds of years ago, Arab, Berber and Jewish traders carried these grains across the Sahara, all the way to Europe, and Europeans gave it this beautiful name, "grains of paradise," just to sell it as a cheaper stand-in for black pepper. It was everywhere — in wine, in beer, in Europe's kitchens. Then the Portuguese found a direct sea route to Asia, and just like that, the world moved on to real black pepper. Grains of paradise and grains of selim quietly went back to being a West African secret. Ours never left — it just stopped being anyone else's.

#### The science, briefly

The warmth in grains of paradise builds slowly, and according to Dr Farrimond's research in *The Science of Spice*, that's down to a compound called paradol — the same one that gives ginger its heat — alongside gingerol for pungency and a bitter, earthy note from humulone. Grains of selim is a different animal altogether: musky, almost smoky, from a compound called fenchone. If you're cooking with grains of paradise at home, it's gentler than black pepper, so use two to three times as much to get the same punch.

There's real modern research behind this one too, not just history: grains of paradise research suggests it may help rev up metabolism, even activate the kind of fat tissue that burns calories for heat. Grains of selim is one of the better-documented African spices for gut and respiratory wellbeing, ginger is one of the most solidly backed spices for digestion, and black pepper is known to help the body absorb more from everything alongside it. For a street-food rub, Yaji does a surprising amount of good — though none of this makes it a treatment for anything, and it's worth reading as background interest rather than a health claim.

#### The blend: Yaji

This is the one that ties Ghana and Nigeria together for me: **Yaji**. It's grains of selim, dry chillies, crushed kuli-kuli (that's crushed peanut puffs), ginger powder, a crumbled stock cube, salt, and black pepper, all combined into one dry rub. Same mix, whether you call it chinchinga or suya.

**Yaji — starting point for your own kitchen**

*Note: this is a starting ratio, not a locked recipe — I'm still testing and adjusting it myself. Treat the ingredient list as the real thing, and the quantities below as a place to begin.*

| Ingredient | Starting amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Crushed kuli-kuli (peanut puffs) | 4 tbsp | Bulk/base — carries everything else |
| Ground grains of selim | 1 tsp | Musky backbone — potent, start low |
| Dry chillies (cayenne, or your own dried chillies) | 1–2 tsp | Adjust to your own heat tolerance |
| Ginger powder | 1 tsp | Warmth |
| Crumbled stock cube | 1 cube | Umami and salt — go easy on added salt if the cube is salty |
| Salt | ½ tsp, to taste | Add after tasting with the stock cube in |
| Black pepper | ½ tsp | Background heat |

**How to test it:** mix the dry ingredients, rub onto a small batch of meat, grill, and taste. Adjust the chillies first for heat, then the grains of selim for how musky you want it, then salt last.

#### The dish

Yaji doesn't stop at skewers. The same mix gets rubbed onto kilishi, that dried, spiced beef jerky you'll find in the same markets, and it's just as often dusted straight onto roasted plantain, corn, or boiled eggs as a street-food seasoning on its own. But the dish it's most famous for is grilled meat skewers — chinchinga, if you grew up in Ghana; suya, if you grew up in Nigeria. Same spice mix, same fire, two names for basically the same street food.

Before anything touches the fire, soak your wooden skewers first — that stops them burning while the meat cooks. Season the meat lightly to start. Once it's on the fire, depending on how tough the cut is, keep spraying it with water or beer as it cooks, to keep it tender. Once it's close to done, take it off, put it in a bowl, and rub the Yaji mix into it properly — then it goes back on the fire just to finish.

#### Why this one stays with me

That's what stays with me about that stall, about my mum cracking those seeds open in her hand: the rest of the world had grains of paradise, grains of selim, for a moment, and then let them go. Ghana never let them go. Nigeria never let them go. And now, on a farm in Sidcup, London, I'm growing my own chillies and spices, trying to carry that same handful forward — for the smell, for the memory, for the market, for her.
