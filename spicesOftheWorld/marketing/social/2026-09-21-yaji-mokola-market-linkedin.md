## Hook line

There was one stall at Mokola Market in Accra my mum always stopped at first — before the fish, before the plantain, before anything else.

## Body

She'd pick up a handful of small, reddish-brown seeds, crack one between her fingers, and let me smell it. Sharp, peppery, warm — nothing like the black pepper I knew from school dinners in the UK. I didn't know it then, but that smell planted the seeds for everything that came after: my love for spices, for farming, for cooking.

That spice was grains of paradise. For a few centuries, it was Europe's cheaper stand-in for black pepper, carried across the Sahara and sold into kitchens across the continent. When Europe found a direct sea route to real black pepper, it moved on. West Africa never did. Ghana is still the world's main grower of it today.

Grains of paradise, together with its cousin grains of selim, is the backbone of Yaji — the dry spice rub behind Ghana's chinchinga and Nigeria's suya. Same blend, two countries, two names, one street food.

I'm building Fudi People around stories like this one: spice history told through where I actually come from, not from a textbook. Right now that means growing chillies and spices on a small farm in Sidcup, London, and building out the blends — including Yaji — one region at a time, starting with Africa.
