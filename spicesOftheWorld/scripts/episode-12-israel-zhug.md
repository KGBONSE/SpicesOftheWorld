## 2. Geography & Origin

Israel shares much of its menu with neighbouring Lebanon, but its palette
keeps evolving — fiery chilli pastes from Yemen, paprika carried in from
Russia and Eastern Europe, harissa from North Africa, and increasingly
Korean, Thai, and Mexican influence brought home by Israeli travellers.
It's one of the most genuinely global spice racks in this whole series.

## 3. Trade & Migration History

Zhug's own story is a good example of how fluid borders actually are here
— it's claimed as Israel's national condiment, but it's just as at home
across the border in Lebanon, and hummus itself is hotly disputed in
origin between the two countries entirely. Rather than pick a side,
Israeli cooking has leaned into the mixing instead — dried hibiscus
flowers for a sharp, cranberry-like fruitiness turn up here in a way
they don't elsewhere in the Middle East, right alongside more familiar
regional staples like caraway on Jewish rye bread and nigella on pittas.

## 4. The Science

Zhug's heat comes from fresh green chillies, but its real backbone is
toasted cardamom, coriander, and cumin ground together before the chilli
even gets pounded in — a proper spice paste underneath the heat, not just
chilli on its own. That layering is what separates zhug from a simple hot
sauce.

That base is doing real health work too. The garlic goes in raw — the
form that keeps its active compound intact, and garlic is about as
well-studied as any ingredient there is for heart health and immunity.
The fresh chilli adds a metabolism and circulation angle, and the toasted
cardamom-coriander-cumin base carries the blood-sugar and digestive
benefits those three are each known for.

## 5. The Blend — Zhug

Roast and grind 1 tsp each cardamom, coriander, and cumin seeds. Pound to
a paste with 2 chopped green chillies and 2 chopped garlic cloves. Combine
with a large bunch each of chopped coriander and flat-leaf parsley, plus
2 tbsp olive oil, and season with salt, pepper, and the juice of half a
lemon.

## Africa Link / Throughline Close

What I like about zhug is that nobody's actually fighting over who
invented it, even while they argue about hummus right next door — it's
just shared, used, loved on both sides of a border that clearly means
less to the food than it does to anything else. That's not so different
from grains of selim showing up under three different names across three
different West African countries. A good spice doesn't really care whose
flag is flying over it.
