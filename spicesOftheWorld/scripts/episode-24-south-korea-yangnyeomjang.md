## 2. Geography & Origin

South Korea — a peninsula squeezed between two much larger neighbours,
China and Japan, and that geography shapes everything about how it
spices food. East Asia as a whole is the world's fourth-largest
spice-producing region today, and most of what grows here is genuinely
mild — Korea leans into that mildness too, but builds real intensity
through technique rather than raw spice quantity.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "East Asia: the world's 4th-largest spice-producing region"
**[B-ROLL]:** Map graphic, Korean market/coastal imagery if available

## 3. Trade & Migration History

Korea's own history of colonization by both China and Japan is exactly
why chilli, ginger, and garlic dominate Korean spicing — all three
arrived through those neighbours rather than growing here natively.
Chilli itself is the same New World native that's turned up in nearly
every region this series has covered, carried here eventually the long
way round. But the flavours that actually make Korean food taste like
nothing else come from technique, not imported spice: fermentation and
preservation, the methods behind kimchi, are what turn borrowed
ingredients into something entirely Korean.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Borrowed spices. Native technique. That's the whole story."

## 4. The Science

Toasted sesame is the backbone of yangnyeomjang, and it's not just
flavour — 40g of hulled sesame seeds daily for two months has been shown
in research to cut LDL cholesterol by around 10% and triglycerides by 8%.
Sesamin and sesamolin, sesame's signature compounds, also carry real
antioxidant and antibacterial properties. Worth the caveat on camera: that
benefit needs regular, substantial intake — tablespoons, not a sprinkle.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "40g sesame seeds daily, 2 months: LDL cholesterol down ~10%"
**[B-ROLL]:** Dry-roasting sesame seeds until golden, close-up texture

## 5. The Blend — Yangnyeomjang

Dry-roast 2 tsp white sesame seeds until golden. Mix with 3 tbsp soy
sauce, 1 tbsp toasted sesame oil, ½ tsp rice vinegar, 2 tsp gochugaru
(dried chilli flakes), ½ tsp caster sugar or honey, 1 crushed garlic
clove, and 1 sliced spring onion. Keeps in the fridge up to a week.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "YANGNYEOMJANG: sesame + soy + sesame oil + rice vinegar + gochugaru + garlic + spring onion"

## 6. The Dish

Yangnyeomjang shows up everywhere in Korean cooking — drizzled over
bibimbap, tossed through japchae glass noodles, stirred into kimchi
jjigae. But its most classic role is the simplest one: a dipping sauce
for mandu, Korean dumplings. Today we're keeping it that way — a straightforward
pork and cabbage mandu filling, wrapped, pan-fried until the base is
crisp, and dipped straight into the sauce.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "MANDU + YANGNYEOMJANG"
