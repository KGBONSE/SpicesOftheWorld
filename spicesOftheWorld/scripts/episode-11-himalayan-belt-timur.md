## 2. Geography & Origin

Nepal, Bhutan, the Tibetan regions — high altitude, harsh seasons, and a
food culture built around wholesome pulses and whatever the foothills
actually provide. In Bhutan specifically, chilli isn't a seasoning, it's
treated as a main ingredient in its own right — a completely different
relationship to heat than anywhere else this series has covered.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Bhutan — chilli as a main ingredient, not a seasoning"
**[B-ROLL]:** Map graphic, Himalayan footage if available

## 3. Trade & Migration History

Fenugreek's own journey already covered a lot of ground by the time it
got here — cattle fodder in Rome, medicine in medieval Europe, a staple
across Iran and West Asia. Up in these mountains it takes on a completely
different role: its bitterness gets deliberately set against the
nuttiness of lentils and pulses, a contrast cooks have leaned on for
generations at altitude where food needs to be both filling and
long-lasting. Timur, the other signature spice here, is a close cousin of
Sichuan pepper — a citrusy, astringent, faintly numbing note that's
native to these mountains specifically, not imported from anywhere.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Timur — a Himalayan cousin of Sichuan pepper"
**[B-ROLL]:** Trade route map animation, close-up of timur/Sichuan pepper if available

## 4. The Science

Timur carries the same numbing, tingling character Sichuan pepper is
known for — a real, physical sensation, not just heat, and one of the
better-studied "surprising" compounds out there. Research on Sichuan
pepper itself (timur's closest relative) has found genuine antioxidant
power and even clinical pain-relief effects — one study found a topical
Sichuan pepper oil eased knee arthritis pain about as well as standard
anti-inflammatory medication.

## 5. The Blend — Timur ko Chhop

Dry-roast 1 tsp timur, or Sichuan pepper if timur isn't available, in a
dry pan over low heat until fragrant. Mix with 2 tbsp red chilli powder
and 1 tsp salt, then grind to a powder.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "TIMUR KO CHHOP: timur + red chilli powder + salt"
**[B-ROLL]:** Dry-roasting the timur, grinding, the finished powder

*(Note: exact quantities straight from the source — no test-cook needed

## 6. The Dish

This one's less a single dish, more the Himalayan equivalent of salt and
pepper on the table — sprinkled over soups, used as a dip for fried meats,
or as a pick-me-up over fried potatoes, noodles, and curries. But it's
also right at home alongside the region's other real signature: ginger-spiced
steamed dumplings, close cousins of the momos this whole belt is known for.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "TIMUR KO CHHOP — the Himalayan salt and pepper"
