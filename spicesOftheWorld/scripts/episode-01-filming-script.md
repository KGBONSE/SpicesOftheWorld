## Session 1 — Talking to Camera
*Indoors, quiet room, facing a window. Set up once, read all five beats
back to back.*

### Intro (~10 sec)
"Today we're going back to West Africa — Ghana and Nigeria — for the
spice blend behind chinchinga and suya, and the two forgotten pepper
substitutes that once rivalled black pepper across Europe."

### 1 · Personal Hook
Growing up in Ghana, there was one stall at Mokola Market my mum, God
bless her soul, would always stop at — before we even got to the fish,
before the plantain, before the Koobi, before anything else.

She'd pick up a handful of these small, reddish-brown seeds, crack one
between her fingers, and let me smell it. Sharp, peppery, warm — nothing
like the black pepper I knew from school dinners back home.

That smell planted the seeds for everything that came after — my love
for spices, for farming, for cooking. And today, I want to take you back
to that exact stall, for the spice that Europe once called "grains of
paradise."

*On-screen: "The spice Europe forgot — West Africa never did" · B-roll:
Mokola Market footage, spice stall close-ups*

### 2 · Geography & Origin
Grains of paradise — they don't come from anywhere fancy, they come from
right here, West Africa. And Ghana, today, is still the main place in
the world growing it.

There's a whole stretch of coastline that used to be called the Grain
Coast, the Pepper Coast, because of how much of this spice moved through
it.

And its cousin, grains of selim, that one travels even further — from
Ethiopia all the way to Ghana, and it's especially loved in southern
Nigeria too.

*On-screen: "Ghana — the world's main producer, today" · B-roll: Map
graphic, West African coastline, farm/polytunnel shots*

### 3 · Trade & Migration History
Here's the thing that gets me — hundreds of years ago, Arab and Berber
and Jewish traders carried these grains across the Sahara, all the way to
Europe, and Europeans gave it this beautiful name, "grains of paradise,"
just to sell it as a cheaper stand-in for black pepper. It was
everywhere, in wine, in beer, in Europe's kitchens.

And then the Portuguese found a direct sea route to Asia, and just like
that, the world moved on to real black pepper — and grains of paradise,
grains of selim, they quietly went back to being a West African secret.
Ours never left, it just stopped being anyone else's.

*On-screen: "Once Europe's pepper substitute — now West Africa's secret"
· B-roll: Trade route map animation, spice market footage*

### 4 · The Science
The warmth in grains of paradise builds slowly, and that's down to a
compound called paradol — the same one that gives ginger its heat.
There's gingerol in there too, for pungency, and a bitter, earthy note
from humulone.

Grains of selim is a different animal altogether — musky, almost smoky,
from a compound called fenchone. And here's a tip if you're cooking with
grains of paradise at home: it's gentler than black pepper, so you'll
want two to three times as much to get the same punch.

And there's real modern research behind this one too, not just history —
grains of paradise has actually been shown to rev up metabolism, even
activate the kind of fat tissue that burns calories for heat. It's not
the only one in the rub with a research file, either: grains of selim is
one of the better-documented African spices for gut and respiratory
health, ginger is about the most solidly-backed spice going for digestion
and nausea, and the black pepper helps your body absorb more from
everything alongside it. For a street-food rub, Yaji does a surprising
amount of good.

*On-screen: "Paradol = ginger's heat compound" / "Use 2–3x more than
black pepper" / "Grains of paradise: metabolism · Grains of selim: gut &
lungs · Ginger: digestion" · B-roll: Close-up of ground spice, grinding
action*

## Session 2 — Kitchen

### 5 · The Blend — Yaji
This is the one that ties Ghana and Nigeria together for me — Yaji. It's
grains of selim, whole Ashanti pepper or any good dry chillies, crushed
kuli-kuli — that's crushed peanut puffs — ginger powder, a crumbled stock
cube, salt, and black pepper, all combined into one dry rub.

Same mix, whether you call it chinchinga or suya.

*On-screen: "YAJI: grains of selim + dry chillies + kuli-kuli + ginger +
stock cube + salt + black pepper" · B-roll: Hands mixing the dry rub,
close-up texture shots*

## Session 3 — Outdoors / Grill
*Skewers soaking, seasoning, grilling. Close-ups of the crust forming
sell this more than wide shots.*

### 6 · The Dish
Yaji doesn't stop at skewers, by the way — the same mix gets rubbed onto
kilishi, that dried, spiced beef jerky you'll find in the same markets,
and it's just as often dusted straight onto roasted plantain, corn, or
boiled eggs as a street-food seasoning on its own. But the one we're
actually making today is the one it's most famous for.

And what Yaji is really for is grilled meat skewers, coated in this dry
rub before they hit the fire — chinchinga, if you grew up in Ghana; suya,
if you grew up in Nigeria. Same spice mix, same fire, two names for
basically the same street food. That's the whole story of this episode
in one bite.

Before anything touches the fire, soak your wooden skewers first — that's
what stops them burning while the meat cooks. Season the meat lightly to
start, then once it's on the fire, depending on how tough the cut is,
keep spraying it with water or beer as it cooks, to keep it tender. Once
it's close to done, take it off, put it in a bowl, and rub the Yaji mix
into it properly — then it goes back on the fire just to finish.

*On-screen: "Yaji also seasons: kilishi (dried beef) · roasted
plantain/corn · boiled eggs" then "CHINCHINGA (Ghana) / SUYA (Nigeria) —
same mix, same fire" · B-roll: Skewers soaking, grilling footage,
spraying water/beer, rubbing Yaji on in the bowl, finished skewers back
on the fire, close-up of the crust*
