# Episode 1 — West Africa: Grains of Paradise, Grains of Selim & Yaji

Ghana and Nigeria, tied together through one real spice blend: **Yaji**, the
marinade behind Ghana's **chinchinga** and Nigeria's **suya** — same spice
mix, two countries, two names (per Kofi — not in the book, this is his own
knowledge). Grounded in `knowledge-base/regional/africa-spice-notes.md` and
the two spice profiles in `knowledge-base/spice-profiles/by-flavour-compound/`
— nothing else below is invented.

## 1. Personal Hook

Growing up in Ghana, there was one stall at Mokola Market my mum, God bless
her soul, would always stop at — before we even got to the fish, before the
plantain, before anything else. She'd pick up a handful of these small,
reddish-brown seeds, crack one between her fingers, and let me smell it.
Sharp, peppery, warm — nothing like the black pepper I knew from school
dinners back home. That smell planted the seeds for everything that came
after — my love for spices, for farming, for cooking. And today, I want to
take you back to that exact stall, for the spice that Europe once called
"grains of paradise."

## 2. Geography & Origin

Grains of paradise — they don't come from anywhere fancy, they come from
right here, West Africa. And Ghana, today, is still the main place in the
world growing it. There's a whole stretch of coastline that used to be
called the Grain Coast, the Pepper Coast, because of how much of this
spice moved through it. And its cousin, grains of selim, that one travels
even further — from Ethiopia all the way to Ghana, and it's especially
loved in southern Nigeria too.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Ghana — the world's main producer, today"
**[B-ROLL]:** Map graphic, West African coastline, farm/polytunnel shots

## 3. Trade & Migration History

Here's the thing that gets me — hundreds of years ago, Arab and Berber and
Jewish traders carried these grains across the Sahara, all the way to
Europe, and Europeans gave it this beautiful name, "grains of paradise,"
just to sell it as a cheaper stand-in for black pepper. It was everywhere,
in wine, in beer, in Europe's kitchens. And then the Portuguese found a
direct sea route to Asia, and just like that, the world moved on to real
black pepper — and grains of paradise, grains of selim, they quietly went
back to being a West African secret. Ours never left, it just stopped
being anyone else's.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Once Europe's pepper substitute — now West Africa's secret"
**[B-ROLL]:** Trade route map animation, spice market footage

## 4. The Science

The warmth in grains of paradise builds slowly, and that's down to a
compound called paradol — the same one that gives ginger its heat. There's
gingerol in there too, for pungency, and a bitter, earthy note from
humulone. Grains of selim is a different animal altogether — musky,
almost smoky, from a compound called fenchone. And here's a tip if you're
cooking with grains of paradise at home: it's gentler than black pepper,
so you'll want two to three times as much to get the same punch.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Paradol = ginger's heat compound" / "Use 2–3x more than black pepper"
**[B-ROLL]:** Close-up of ground spice, grinding action

## 5. The Blend — Yaji

This is the one that ties Ghana and Nigeria together for me — Yaji. It's
grains of selim, whole Ashanti pepper, crushed kuli-kuli — that's crushed
peanut puffs — ginger powder, cayenne pepper flakes, a crumbled stock
cube, salt, and black pepper, all combined into one dry rub. Same mix,
whether you call it chinchinga or suya.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "YAJI: grains of selim + Ashanti pepper + kuli-kuli + ginger + cayenne + stock cube + salt + black pepper"

### Test Batch Card — Yaji v1 (untested, for your kitchen)

Not from the book or any source — a starting ratio based on typical
suya/yaji proportions, sized small on purpose so a bad batch costs
nothing. Test, taste, adjust, and log what you change below before this
locks in for filming.

| Ingredient | v1 amount | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Crushed kuli-kuli (peanut puffs) | 4 tbsp | Bulk/base — carries everything else |
| Ground grains of selim | 1 tsp | Musky backbone — potent, start low |
| Ground Ashanti pepper (or whole, cracked) | 1 tsp | Citrus-tinged heat |
| Cayenne pepper flakes | 1–2 tsp | Adjust to your real heat tolerance |
| Ginger powder | 1 tsp | Warmth |
| Crumbled stock cube | 1 cube | Umami/salt — reduce added salt if cube is salty |
| Salt | ½ tsp, to taste | Add after tasting with the stock cube in |
| Black pepper | ½ tsp | Background heat |

**How to test:** mix dry, rub onto a small batch of meat (2–3 skewers'
worth), grill, taste. Adjust cayenne first (heat), then grains of
selim/Ashanti pepper (how musky/citrusy vs. straightforward you want it),
then salt last.

## 6. The Dish

And what Yaji is really for is grilled meat skewers, coated in this dry
rub before they hit the fire — chinchinga, if you grew up in Ghana; suya,
if you grew up in Nigeria. Same spice mix, same fire, two names for
basically the same street food. That's the whole story of this episode in
one bite.
