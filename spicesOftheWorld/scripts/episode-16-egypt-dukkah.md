## 2. Geography & Origin

Egypt's ports were a gateway to Eastern spice riches long before its
mercantile peak in the Middle Ages, and Alexandria was the main hub of the
entire ancient spice trade until the balance of power shifted to Rome.
And yet, for all that history, the modern Egyptian palette stays notably
simpler than its Levantine neighbours — cumin does the everyday heavy
lifting here, the way it does in Ghana's own kitchens.

## 3. Trade & Migration History

This is the one to sit with: Ancient Egyptians founded the spice trade
itself, importing goods from Africa and Arabia as early as 3000 BCE —
which makes Egypt the literal ancient hinge connecting African and Middle
Eastern spice trade, long before either region looked anything like it
does today. That's a direct thread back to the Maghreb episode earlier in
this series — two ends of the same African coastline, telling one

## 4. The Science

Dukkah is built almost entirely on toasting — hazelnuts and almonds
roasted separately, then coriander, cumin, and sesame dry-fried until
their pyrazine compounds develop that nutty, roasted character this
whole series keeps coming back to. It's proof that a spice blend doesn't
need heat or rarity to be genuinely complex — just the right technique
applied properly to simple ingredients.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "No rarity, no heat — just the right technique"
**[B-ROLL]:** Hazelnuts and almonds roasting, spices dry-frying

## 5. The Blend — Dukkah

Roast 150g hazelnuts for 10 minutes and 150g whole almonds for 8 minutes
at 200°C. Dry-fry 1 tbsp coriander seeds, 1 tbsp cumin seeds, and 3 tbsp
sesame seeds over high heat for 3 minutes. Grind everything together with
a pinch of salt.

## 6. The Dish

Dukkah is an everyday condiment, served with olive oil and bread for
dipping, or scattered over roasted meats and hummus. But cumin's own
everyday role in Egyptian cooking shows up biggest in koshari — the
national dish, built on lentils, rice, and pasta, topped with a spiced
tomato sauce and crispy onions.
