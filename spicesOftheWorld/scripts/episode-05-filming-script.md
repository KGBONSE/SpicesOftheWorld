### 2 · Geography & Origin
Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya — the Maghreb. The book calls it the
portal where Africa meets the Middle East and Europe, and you can taste
that in the food — sweet and mild overall, cumin doing the everyday heavy
lifting, cinnamon bringing warmth to fruit-laden tagines.

*On-screen: "The Maghreb — where Africa meets the Middle East and Europe" ·
B-roll: map graphic*

### 3 · Trade & Migration History
Cumin might be the oldest spice we've covered — there's evidence of it in
Egyptian pyramids, more than 5,000 years old. The Romans loved it so much
Pliny the Elder called it the "king of condiments." From the 7th century,
Arab traders carried it along the caravan routes into North Africa and east
as far as Iran, India, Indonesia, China — it's in baharat, garam masala,
panch phoran, ras el hanout, all the same seed. And here's the twist for
this region specifically — for centuries the Maghreb wasn't just receiving
influence, Berbers themselves ruled much of Spain as Al-Andalus. This place
has always moved in more than one direction.

*On-screen: "Cumin — 5,000+ years, Egyptian pyramids to Pliny the Elder" ·
B-roll: historical map/timeline animation*

### 4 · The Science
Cumin's flavour comes from cuminaldehyde — musky, spicy, and it's rarely
found anywhere else, except, interestingly, in cinnamon too. That's part of
why the two work so well together here — cinnamon's own compound,
cinnamaldehyde, adds an earthier warming depth right alongside it. Toast
the cumin seeds first, bruise them, dry-fry them, and you get roasted notes
you just don't get from the raw seed.

Both of these carry real research weight too — cumin's been linked to
genuine digestive and metabolic benefits, and even a small daily amount of
true cinnamon shows up in studies improving blood sugar and cholesterol.

*On-screen: "Cumin + cinnamon share real chemistry — toast the seeds
first" / "Research backs both spices for digestion, blood sugar and
cholesterol" · B-roll: seeds toasting, close-up*

### 5 · The Blend — Harissa
Soak 100 grams of dried red chillies — Baklouti if you can get them — in
hot water for 30 minutes. Then blend with 3 to 4 garlic cloves, salt, lemon
juice, ground cumin, ground coriander, ground caraway, and olive oil, into
a paste.

*On-screen: "HARISSA: 100g dried red chillies + 3–4 garlic cloves + salt +
lemon juice + cumin + coriander + caraway + olive oil" · B-roll: chillies
soaking, blending into paste*

### 6 · The Dish — Tagine + Harissa
*(Can be voiced over the footage.)*

Harissa turns up all over Maghrebi cooking — stirred into couscous broth,
spooned into shakshuka's tomato-and-pepper sauce, rubbed onto merguez
before it hits the grill. But the pairing that shows off cumin and cinnamon
best is the one we're doing today.

Stir that harissa through a tagine already built on cumin and cinnamon —
fruit, meat, that slow-cooked warmth — and you've got the Maghreb on a
plate. Harissa doesn't need its own dish, really — it's the thing that
finishes everything else.

*On-screen: "Harissa also lives in: couscous · shakshuka · merguez" then
"TAGINE + HARISSA" · B-roll: tagine simmering, harissa being stirred in*
