South Africa, Mozambique, Botswana, Zimbabwe — Southern Africa. South Africa calls itself the "Rainbow Nation," and nowhere is that truer than in the food. Dutch colonisation brought Indonesian-influenced spicing into Cape Malay cooking; Indian indentured labourers under British rule turned Durban into a genuine global contender for curry.

This region has some of the most direct, traceable colonial spice-trade history we've covered — Dutch, British, Portuguese, and Indonesian/Indian influence, all layered into one cuisine. Mozambique carries Portuguese-influenced piri piri; Botswana and Zimbabwe use spice far more sparingly than their neighbours. But Durban is the real story here — its curry tradition isn't inspired by India, it's literally Indian cooking, transplanted through indentured labour and never left.

Coriander is the spice tying this one together, and it's ancient — an 8,000-year-old stockpile was found in a cave in Israel. Its flavour comes mostly from linalool, that lilac-floral note. And here's a nice callback — coriander bridges with cumin, the spice from our Maghreb episode, through a shared compound called cymene. Different regions, same chemistry.

Coriander's picking up real research interest too — early studies point to genuine blood sugar and cholesterol benefits, alongside cumin's own digestive and metabolic backing from a couple of episodes ago.
