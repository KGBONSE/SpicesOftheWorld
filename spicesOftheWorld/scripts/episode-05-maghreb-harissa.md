## 2. Geography & Origin

Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya — the Maghreb. The book calls it the
portal where Africa meets the Middle East and Europe, and you can taste
that in the food — sweet and mild overall, cumin doing the everyday
heavy lifting, cinnamon bringing warmth to fruit-laden tagines.

## 3. Trade & Migration History

Cumin might be the oldest spice we've covered — there's evidence of it in
Egyptian pyramids, more than 5,000 years old. The Romans loved it so much
Pliny the Elder called it the "king of condiments." From the 7th century,
Arab traders carried it along the caravan routes into North Africa and
east as far as Iran, India, Indonesia, China — it's in baharat, garam
masala, panch phoran, ras el hanout, all the same seed. And here's the
twist for this region specifically — for centuries the Maghreb wasn't
just receiving influence, Berbers themselves ruled much of Spain as
Al-Andalus. This place has always moved in more than one direction.

## 4. The Science

Cumin's flavour comes from cuminaldehyde — musky, spicy, and it's rarely
found anywhere else, except, interestingly, in cinnamon too. That's part
of why the two work so well together here — cinnamon's own compound,
cinnamaldehyde, adds an earthier warming depth right alongside it. Toast
the cumin seeds first, bruise them, dry-fry them, and you get roasted
notes you just don't get from the raw seed.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Cumin + cinnamon share real chemistry — toast the seeds first"

## 5. The Blend — Harissa

Soak 100 grams of dried red chillies — Baklouti if you can get them — in
hot water for 30 minutes. Then blend with 3 to 4 garlic cloves, salt,
lemon juice, ground cumin, ground coriander, ground caraway, and olive
oil, into a paste.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "HARISSA: 100g dried red chillies + 3–4 garlic cloves + salt + lemon juice + cumin + coriander + caraway + olive oil"
**[B-ROLL]:** Chillies soaking, blending into paste

*(Note: chilli and garlic quantities are exact from the source — the
ground spices are "to taste," not measured. Worth test-cooking to lock in
your own ratio there.)*

## 6. The Dish

Harissa turns up all over Maghrebi cooking — stirred into couscous broth,
spooned into shakshuka's tomato-and-pepper sauce, rubbed onto merguez
before it hits the grill. But the pairing that shows off cumin and
cinnamon best is the one we're doing today.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Harissa also lives in: couscous · shakshuka · merguez"

Stir that harissa through a tagine already built on cumin and cinnamon —
fruit, meat, that slow-cooked warmth — and you've got the Maghreb on a
plate. Harissa doesn't need its own dish, really — it's the thing that
finishes everything else.

## Africa Link / Throughline Close

What I like about the Maghreb is it breaks the story a little — paprika
and allspice turning up here came from the Americas, not the other way
round, and Berbers ruling part of Spain means this corner of Africa was
exporting its own influence outward too, not just receiving everyone
else's. That's the same thing I'm trying to do from London — not just
carrying Africa's spices somewhere new, but making sure the story travels
with them, the way it always has.
