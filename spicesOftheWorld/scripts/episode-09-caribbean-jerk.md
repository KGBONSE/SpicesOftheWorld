## 2. Geography & Origin

The Caribbean — Jamaica, and the wider West Indies. This is where
Christopher Columbus ended up while he was actually searching for the
source of black pepper — in the 1490s, he stumbled onto allspice in
Jamaica instead. Allspice is the only spice in the world grown exclusively
in the western hemisphere — nowhere else on earth grows it natively.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Columbus was looking for black pepper. He found allspice instead."
**[B-ROLL]:** Map graphic, allspice berries close-up

## 3. Trade & Migration History

What followed Columbus was centuries of empire-building that nearly wiped
out the Caribbean's indigenous peoples, and turned the West Indies into a
new spice-trade hub in its own right. That history is exactly why
Caribbean food is the genuine melting pot it is today — Amerindian,
African, Creole, Spanish, Dutch, Portuguese, British, Latin American,
Persian, South Asian, and Indonesian influences, all in one place. Some of
that is trade. Some of it isn't. Turmeric, coriander, and fenugreek are
in Caribbean curry powder today because of indentured South Asian
labourers, the same story that built Durban's curry tradition two
episodes ago. And calabash nutmeg — the spice Episode 7 closed on — got
here a different way entirely, carried through the transatlantic slave
trade, and renamed "Jamaican nutmeg" once it landed. Same thread, same
honesty about it.

## 4. The Science

Allspice gets its name for a reason — it genuinely tastes like cinnamon,
clove, and nutmeg all at once, in one single berry. That's down to
caryophyllene, a spicy, woody, drying compound — and here's the callback:
it's the same compound that gives black pepper its own woody edge.
Different plant, different hemisphere, same chemistry, same trick
Columbus's own pepper hunt accidentally proved four centuries early.

Nutmeg itself — one of the three flavours you're tasting in that one
berry — has real antioxidant and antibacterial research behind it too,
though it comes with a genuine caution worth saying out loud: a little
truly goes a long way, since too much nutmeg is actually harmful, not
just overpowering.

## 5. The Blend — Jamaican Jerk Rub

Grind 2 tsp allspice, 1 tsp whole black peppercorns, and ½ tsp cloves.
Combine with 1 tbsp chipotle or cayenne powder, 1 tsp paprika, 1 tsp
grated nutmeg, 1 tsp ground ginger, ½ tsp ground cinnamon, 2 tsp onion
powder, 2 tsp garlic powder, 1 tsp dried thyme, 2 tbsp brown sugar, and 2
tsp sea salt.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "JERK RUB: allspice + black pepper + cloves + chipotle/cayenne + paprika + nutmeg + ginger + cinnamon + onion + garlic + thyme + brown sugar + salt"

## 6. The Dish

That same curry powder — turmeric, coriander, fenugreek — turns up across
the islands in West Indian curries, and roasted nigella seeds season
breads and vegetable dishes the same way they do back in West Africa. But
jerk is the one this rub was actually built for.

**[ON-SCREEN TEXT]:** "Jerk rub also builds: West Indian curry · nigella-seed flatbreads"

Jerk chicken — marinated in this exact rub, traditionally over pimento
wood for a smoky finish, though a regular grill or oven gets you most of
the way there. Invented in Jamaica, adapted island to island, chicken,
fish, pork, or beef depending where you are — same rub underneath all of
it.

Here's the thing I keep coming back to — Episode 7 wasn't a detour, it was
a set-up. Calabash nutmeg didn't choose to come here, but it did, and once
it landed, it became "Jamaican nutmeg" — a genuinely Caribbean spice now,
with an African root nobody's allowed to forget. And it's not the only
one. Look at that list again: African is right there, next to Creole, next
to Spanish, next to South Asian, all fused into one plate. Not every
spice route out of Africa was chosen. But wherever it landed, it never
stopped being part of the story — same as Ghana, same as Zanzibar, same
