# Paprika

**Botanical name:** Capsicum annuum
**Taste profile:** Bittersweet, earthy, fruity
**Also known as:** Hungarian pepper, pimentón
**Flavour group:** Toasty Pyrazines

## Origin & History
Capsicum peppers probably originated in Mexico, where recent cave findings suggest they were eaten as far back as 7000 BCE. Christopher Columbus shipped peppers back to Europe from the Caribbean in the late 15th century. Spanish monks began drying and grinding the fruits, and one of the world's best smoked paprikas is still made in Extremadura's La Vera valley using ancient stone mills. The Ottoman Turks introduced peppers to Hungary, and by the mid-1800s Hungarians had adopted paprika as their national spice, key to goulash. French chef Georges Escoffier is credited with popularizing paprika internationally after shipping it from Hungary to his Monte Carlo restaurant in 1879.

## Flavour Chemistry
Pyrazine combinations provide earthy, smoky depth, many developed during drying or smoking. Sugars and ethyl acetate contribute sweet, rum-like fruitiness; isovaleraldehyde and acetoine add fatty, buttery notes; citric acid gives tartness.

## Pairing Logic
- Wattle, black cardamom, sesame, and ajwain pair with related pyrazine compounds.
- Cinnamon, allspice, caraway, and saffron enhance the sweet side.
- Cacao and saffron give prominence to paprika's rich underlying flavours.
- Tamarind, coriander, and ginger boost tanginess through acidity.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Appreciated for earthy, smoky flavour, spicy heat, and natural sugar sweetness — very mild varieties can add colour without significantly changing a dish's flavour. High in carotenoids (similar to carrots), which dissolve well in oil, so frying with oil at the start of cooking helps spread both colour and flavour.

## Blends Featuring Paprika
Jamaican jerk rub, Chimichurri, BBQ rub

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Heart health:** paprika's carotenoids have lowered LDL and total cholesterol in a 12-week human study.
- **Eye health:** its lutein and zeaxanthin content is linked to a lower risk of cataracts and age-related macular degeneration.
- **Inflammation/pain:** capsaicin content (higher in hotter varieties) has anti-inflammatory and pain-relief properties.
- **Blood sugar:** capsaicin may also improve insulin sensitivity and help regulate blood sugar.
- Sweet/mild paprika has much less capsaicin than hot varieties, so the pain/blood-sugar angle applies most to hotter paprikas.
