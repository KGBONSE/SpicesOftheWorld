# Wattle

**Botanical name:** Acacia victoriae (most common species used)
**Taste profile:** Roasted, woody, musty
**Also known as:** Gundabluey wattle
**Flavour group:** Toasty Pyrazines

## Origin & History
Wattle has been a staple food for Aboriginal Australians for at least 4,000 years. Only a handful of the hundreds of acacia species found in Australia have edible seeds, and some others are toxic — over thousands of years, Indigenous communities identified the edible varieties, eating seeds fresh or crushing dried, roasted seeds into flour using grinding stones. Rising commercial interest has led to small-scale plantations, though much of the crop is still wild-harvested. As "bush food" increasingly appears on Australian restaurant menus, wattle's importance as a culinary spice continues to grow.

## Flavour Chemistry
A water-soluble pyrazine gives wattle a bitter, earthy, roasted-coffee flavour with hints of cocoa. An oil-soluble pyrazine adds sweet, nutty, charred-wood character with popcorn notes. Citral and bitter phenols are also present.

## Pairing Logic
- Sumac, tamarind, juniper, and carob enhance the roasted, earthy, bitter flavours.
- Cinnamon, paprika, cacao, and cumin complement the sweet, woody, popcorn aromas.
- Lemon myrtle, lemongrass, dill, and caraway foreground the lemony component.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Nutty, roasted coffee-like taste with a lightly charred woody aroma and hints of popcorn and sweet citrus — doesn't need frying or toasting, since roasted flavours are already generated during processing. Cooking in fat decreases bitter phenol levels and highlights lemony, nutty notes; cooking in water-based liquids brings out bitter, roasted-coffee-cocoa flavours instead. Can be used like ground coffee in an espresso machine.
