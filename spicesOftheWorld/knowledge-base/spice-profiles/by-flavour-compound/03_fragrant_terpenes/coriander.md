# Coriander

**Botanical name:** Coriandrum sativum
**Taste profile:** Citrus, floral, warming
**Also known as:** Cilantro; sometimes mistakenly called Indian, Chinese, or Japanese parsley
**Flavour group:** Fragrant Terpenes

## Origin & History
An 8,000-year-old stockpile of coriander discovered in Israel's Nahal Hemar cave, alongside evidence from Egyptian tombs, points to its origins as a commercial crop in the Near East. Greeks and Romans used it as medicine and meat preservative. It reached India via Persia around 2,000 years ago, and within four centuries had spread from China to Anglo-Saxon Britain. European settlers carried it to North America, where it naturalized widely. By the 18th century it had fallen out of European culinary fashion, becoming mostly limited to gin distilling and beer brewing — it remains a popular flavouring in Belgian beers today.

## Flavour Chemistry
Linalool, with a lilac-floral character, dominates coriander's flavour profile, alongside milder terpenes including pinene, cymene, and limonene.

## Pairing Logic
- Cardamom, nutmeg, and mace bring floral, warming sweetness.
- Black pepper enhances the woody pinene notes.
- Cumin bridges through shared cymene, balancing coriander's floral citrus.
- Ginger, lemongrass, and caraway add fruity/peppery complexity via limonene.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
A versatile spice, often paired with earthy cumin to form the backbone of savoury blends worldwide. Leaving seeds untoasted preserves green, floral character; dry frying develops nutty, earthy pyrazines instead.

## Blends Featuring Coriander
Zhug, Dukkah, Durban curry masala, Malaysian fish curry paste, Chimichurri

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Blood sugar:** coriander seed extracts have shown a hypoglycemic effect, helping glucose tolerance in animal studies.
- **Heart health:** shown to shift cholesterol balance favourably (lower LDL, higher HDL) in animal research, and acts as a mild diuretic that may help lower blood pressure.
- **Antioxidant/anti-inflammatory:** rich in antioxidants; linalool, its dominant compound, has shown anxiety-reducing effects in human studies.
- Most of the strongest evidence is still animal-based — keep any spoken claim to "may help" territory.
