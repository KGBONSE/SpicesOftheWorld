# Mastic

**Botanical name:** Pistacia lentiscus
**Taste profile:** Pine-like, resinous, woody
**Also known as:** Arabic gum (not gum arabic), mastiha, lentisk
**Flavour group:** Fragrant Terpenes

## Origin & History
Mastic grows only on the Greek island of Chios, cultivated there for over 2,500 years. Ancient Greeks and Romans chewed it as a breath freshener — it's the origin of the word "masticate." When the Genoese took Chios from the Venetians in 1346, they protected the islanders from pirates in exchange for a monopoly over the mastic trade. The Ottomans seized control of the trade in 1566 until Chios became part of Greece in 1913. Today, 24 remaining mastic-producing villages (*mastichochoria*) still harvest the resin using time-honoured methods, and Chios mastic now carries Protected Designation of Origin status.

## Flavour Chemistry
Pinene makes up as much as 80% of mastic's chemical profile, giving it its subtle pine aroma. Myrcene adds peppery, light balsamic character; linalool contributes floral notes; caryophyllene brings woody bitterness.

## Pairing Logic
- Nutmeg complements the dominant pine flavour with its own floral, woody notes.
- Coriander and allspice boost pepperiness via shared myrcene.
- Rose and cardamom draw out mastic's floral aromas.
- Cinnamon and clove enhance woodiness via caryophyllene.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
In Turkey mastic flavours Turkish delight; in Lebanon it's used in eggless ice cream scented with rosewater; in Egypt it flavours red meat and poultry dishes, often alongside cardamom. Best ground before use and mixed with sugar, salt, or flour to prevent it sticking to the grinder and to help disperse it through a dish. Note: adding whole mastic directly to a watery broth risks it clumping into a sticky, plastic-like mass, since its myrcene molecules form long polymer chains in water.
