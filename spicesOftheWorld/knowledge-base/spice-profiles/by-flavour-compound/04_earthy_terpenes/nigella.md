# Nigella

**Botanical name:** Nigella sativa
**Taste profile:** Mild, peppery, herbaceous
**Also known as:** Love-in-a-mist, black onion seeds, black cumin, black caraway
**Flavour group:** Earthy Terpenes

## Origin & History
Reputedly found in King Tutankhamun's tomb, nigella has been cultivated for its seeds for over 3,000 years. Its name derives from Latin *nigellus*/*niger* (black). Ancient Greeks and Arabs valued its curative properties; an old Arab proverb calls it "a remedy for all diseases except death," while the Old Testament calls it *ketzah*. The Roman physician Galen prescribed it for colds — a use still practised today, wrapping a spoonful of seeds in muslin to inhale for a blocked nose.

## Flavour Chemistry
Cymene gives nigella its earthy, fresh, turpentine-like aroma; smaller amounts of pinene and limonene add mild terpene character. The main flavour compound, nigellone, forms from thymoquinone — a medicinal compound also found in oregano, which explains why many people detect an oregano-like similarity in nigella's taste.

## Pairing Logic
- Ajwain, cumin, and nutmeg enhance the fresh cymene notes.
- Black pepper and cinnamon add depth via pinene.
- Coriander and caraway pair with the fruity limonene side.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
A punchy spice with mild heat and a bitter, herby, charred-onion character, widely used in Indian cooking and valued for pickling due to its preservative properties. The seeds are hard, with flavourful oils locked in tiny capsules — grinding then toasting in a dry pan helps release them and generates additional pyrazine flavour compounds.

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Heart/metabolic:** supplementation has improved lipid profiles (lower triglycerides, LDL, total cholesterol; higher HDL) in research on dyslipidemia.
- **Anti-inflammatory/antioxidant:** thymoquinone, nigella's main bioactive compound, reduces oxidative stress and inflammation, and supports immune function.
- **Broader applications:** preliminary support found for diabetes, hypertension, obesity, asthma, allergic rhinitis, and skin conditions like atopic dermatitis.
- One of the better-researched spices on this list, though larger human trials are still called for.
