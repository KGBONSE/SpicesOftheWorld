# Black Pepper

**Botanical name:** Piper nigrum
**Taste profile:** Hot, spicy, citrusy
**Also known as:** Peppercorn
**Flavour group:** Pungent Compounds

## Origin & History
Black pepper is native to southern India and has been cultivated and traded for over 3,500 years. In the 4th century BCE, following Alexander the Great's reach into India, black pepper was carried to Europe along newly established trade routes and quickly became commercially important — Arab traders held a monopoly on its transport to Europe. By the Middle Ages black peppercorns were a culinary status symbol and were even accepted as currency. The Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama set off to find and control the spice's source, discovering a sea route to southwest India in 1498; Portugal controlled the black pepper trade for the next century, before losing it to the Dutch in the 17th century, who in turn lost it to Britain in the 18th, when the British Empire took control of the tropical spice trade.

## Flavour Chemistry
Piperine, a pungent alkaloid, produces black pepper's warming quality. More subtle flavours come from terpene compounds including woody rotundone, pine-scented pinene, lemony limonene, spicy myrcene, floral linalool, and fresh, green phellandrene.

## Pairing Logic
- Chilli, mustard, Sichuan pepper, and ginger create rounded, complex heat with other pungent spices.
- Bay, black cardamom, and nutmeg foreground coniferous pinene notes.
- Cardamom, lemon myrtle, and turmeric combine with the citrus limonene side.
- Cinnamon, anise, allspice, and anardana pair especially well with the myrcene notes — surprisingly good matches for pink peppercorns.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Produces a pungent heat that is neither flavour nor fragrance but a pain-like sensation, similar to chilli heat, alongside a woody, floral, citrus aroma. Subtle terpene compounds break down and evaporate quickly when exposed to air, so grinding whole peppercorns immediately before use preserves the fullest complexity; pre-ground pepper is only good for adding heat, not aroma.
