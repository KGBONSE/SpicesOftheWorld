# Ginger

**Botanical name:** Zingiber officinale
**Taste profile:** Hot, citrusy, woody
**Also known as:** Ginger root, Canton ginger
**Flavour group:** Pungent Compounds

## Origin & History
Ginger was one of the first Asian spices to reach Europe, carried by Arab traders to ancient Greece and Rome from around the 4th century BCE. Greeks prescribed it for stomach complaints, and Romans used it in sauces and aromatic salt. By the 9th century CE, dried ginger was an everyday European condiment, and by the Middle Ages it was widely used in both savoury and sweet cooking (notably gingerbread) and to flavour beer and ale. By the 13th century, ginger was being grown in East and West Africa, and by the 16th century it was being cultivated in Jamaica, which still holds a reputation for producing quality ginger today.

## Flavour Chemistry
Gingerols give ginger its hot, pungent, spicy character. Linalool and geraniol contribute floral, sweet notes; citral adds herbal, eucalyptus-adjacent citrus; cineole brings mildly medicinal, penetrating freshness. Zingiberene, a terpene compound, carries ginger's characteristic aroma.

## Pairing Logic
- Chilli and black pepper add depth with additional pungent heat.
- Cinnamon, nutmeg, and cacao pair with the floral linalool/geraniol side.
- Lemongrass, lemon myrtle, and coriander enhance the zesty citral notes.
- Bay and cardamom draw out penetrating freshness via cineole.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Has a hot-spice, citrusy, woody taste; the dried form is stronger and more aromatic, commonly used in baking and spice blends, while fresh ginger dominates Asian cuisine. Peeling fresh ginger shortly before use retains its complex flavour profile; the longer ginger is cooked, the gentler its heat becomes as gingerols and shogaols break down into milder zingerone. When dried, ginger's heat pungency actually increases even as roughly a fifth of its flavour molecules evaporate — the drying process converts gingerol into shogaol, which has twice the heat but fewer citrus notes.

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Nausea relief:** ginger's best-established, most clinically consistent benefit — multiple trials confirm it reduces nausea and vomiting; the NHS itself recommends it.
- **Anti-inflammatory/antioxidant:** gingerol and shogaol compounds show strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity.
- **Digestive health:** supports gastric motility, helping IBS and general digestive comfort.
- **Menstrual pain:** shown in research to be about as effective as common pain relievers (mefenamic acid, ibuprofen) for period pain.
- **Blood sugar:** supplementation has meaningfully reduced fasting blood sugar and HbA1c in people with type 2 diabetes over 8–13 weeks.
- One of the most robustly-evidenced spices in this whole knowledge base — safe to lean on confidently on camera.
