# Chilli

**Botanical names:** Capsicum annuum, C. frutescens, and several other species
**Taste profile:** Hot, pungent, fruity
**Also known as:** Chilli pepper, red pepper, hot pepper, chile, chili
**Flavour group:** Pungent Compounds

## Origin & History
Evidence suggests chillies were being cultivated as early as 5000 BCE in South America. According to the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés, the Aztecs cultivated a vast range of chillies for ritual use and to flavour chocolate beverages. Columbus brought the newly discovered spice back to Spain at the end of the 15th century; he wrongly assumed chillies were related to peppercorns because of their heat, and the name has stuck ever since. Portuguese traders transported the spice to their colony in Goa, India, and onward to Asian and African trading posts, where chillies rapidly replaced black pepper as the hot spice of choice. In 1912, pharmacologist William Scoville devised the Scoville Heat Index to measure chilli pungency — now increasingly replaced by more accurate lab-based measures.

## Flavour Chemistry
Capsaicin drives the fiery, numbing pungency. Esters add fruity, sweet, waxy notes, most appreciated in milder and/or fresh chillies; undecanol and limonene contribute floral, citrus character; drying and smoking create new flavour compounds, notably earthy, toasty pyrazines and nutty, bready furfural.

## Pairing Logic
- Mustard, black pepper, grains of paradise, and Sichuan pepper create rounded, complex heat.
- Cinnamon, allspice, caraway, and ajwain enhance the fruity ester side of mild/fresh chillies.
- Coriander, ginger, lemongrass, and turmeric draw out citrus/floral qualities.
- Cacao, cumin, sesame, and turmeric complement the earthy, roasted flavours of dried/smoked chillies.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Appears in countless cuisines worldwide, valued as much for flavour as heat, particularly in Mexican cooking. Dried chillies have roughly double the capsaicin concentration of fresh, since drying concentrates the compound; removing the inner white pith (where most capsaicin resides) is the effective way to reduce heat, more so than removing seeds alone. Less fat in a dish increases perceived heat, since capsaicin dissolves in oil (though some related capsaicinoids do dissolve in water).

### Chilli Varieties Reference
**Fresh:**
- **Jalapeño** — medium-hot; used fresh, roasted, or pickled; called *cuaresmeño* fresh in Mexico, *jalapeño* refers to the pickled form
- **Scotch bonnet** — related to habanero, extremely hot, deep fruity flavour; known in Guyana as "ball of fire"; preferred chilli of the Caribbean
- **Bird's eye** (Thai chilli) — fiery, ubiquitous in Asian cooking, used fresh or dried

**Dried:**
- **Cascabel** — cherry-tomato-shaped, nicknamed "rattle chilli" for its rattling seeds; tropical sweetness without excessive heat
- **Ancho** — dried poblano; sweet, fruity, mild, hint of tobacco; good soaked/stuffed or blitzed into mole sauce
- **Guajillo** — dark, sun-dried form of the mirasol chilli; smoky sweetness with mild heat; classic in Mexican tamales, enchiladas, salsas
- **Arbol** — slender, sun-dried; bright, fiery, smoky; fried in oil or crushed into flakes
- **Kashmiri** — deep crimson colour, only mildly spicy despite appearance; essential to rogan josh and Kashmiri biryani
- **Mulato** — related to ancho, smokier taste, meaty texture; toasted/soaked/ground into paste
- **Habanero** — bright orange, intensely fruity aroma; essential to cochinita pibil (slow-cooked Mexican pork)
- **Pasilla** — medium-hot, complex sweet flavour resembling liquorice; nicknamed "little raisin"; works in sweet recipes like chocolate cake
- **Piri piri** 🌍 — small but fiery (milder than true bird's-eye); explicitly used in African cuisine to spice meat dishes; also known as African bird's eye

**Ground:**
- **Smoked chipotle** — smoke-dried ripe jalapeños; smoky with chocolate-like sweetness
- **Cayenne** — clean, sharp heat; important in Indian and South American cuisine
- **Espelette** — legally protected variety from the Basque commune of Espelette, France; citrusy flavour, medium heat; used in meat cures, piperade, fish stew

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Heart health:** large population studies link regular chilli/capsaicin consumption to lower cardiovascular disease risk and lower all-cause mortality.
- **Weight management:** capsaicin can boost energy expenditure and reduce appetite, aiding fat loss alongside a healthy diet.
- **Pain relief:** capsaicin acts on TRPV1 receptors, giving it real analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects, useful for chronic pain conditions.
- **Neuroprotection:** shown to be neuroprotective and cognitively supportive in research, with possible relevance to Alzheimer's/Parkinson's.
- **Caution:** excessive intake can irritate the gut and worsen reflux — worth a balancing line given how central chilli is to Fudi People's brand.
