# Cacao

**Botanical name:** Theobroma cacao
**Taste profile:** Earthy, floral, bittersweet
**Also known as:** Cocoa
**Flavour group:** Fruity Aldehydes

## Origin & History
Evidence shows cacao was being used by the Olmec people of southern Mexico as early as 1500 BCE. By 600 BCE, the Olmec had introduced cacao to the Maya of Yucatan, who used it as a source of nourishment and traded it to the Aztecs, who turned the beans into a thick, unsweetened drink. When the Spanish invaded Yucatan in the early 16th century, they recognized cacao as a precious commodity and began sweetening it with cane sugar. The first recorded shipment of cacao beans to Europe reached Spain in 1585, and within a century chocolate beverages were being consumed across Europe, well before coffee and tea. The first solid chocolate bar was made by Fry's of Bristol, England, in 1847. Today more than 50% of the world's cacao crop comes from Ivory Coast and Ghana.

## Flavour Chemistry
Cacao contains around 600 flavour compounds. Pyrazines, theobromine, and caffeine contribute bitter, roasted-nut, earthy character — bitterness is partly produced by caffeine and theobromine converting to bitter pyrazines on roasting. Isovaleraldehyde and related aldehydes add chocolatey, fatty, peach-like notes; ketones bring sweet honey and floral qualities; alcohols and esters add fruity, herby, flowery complexity.

## Pairing Logic
- Wattle, black pepper, sesame, and cumin enhance the roasted, nutty side.
- Vanilla, mahleb, and mace boost sweet chocolate flavours.
- Coriander, chilli, bay, and ginger stand up to bitterness while enhancing fruit and floral notes.
- Liquorice, cinnamon, allspice, and sweet paprika bolster the sweet honey aroma.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Cacao nibs are earthy, bitter, and fragrant, with a nut-like crunch — unlike chocolate, they don't melt without grinding to a paste. Unroasted ("raw") nibs are notably bitter; roasting at 140°C (275°F) for 10–15 minutes removes some bitterness and develops nutty pyrazine flavours.
