# Galangal

**Botanical name:** Alpinia galanga
**Taste profile:** Warming, pungent, peppery
**Also known as:** Greater galangal, Laos root, Thai ginger, Siamese ginger
**Flavour group:** Penetrating Terpenes

## Origin & History
The Greek philosopher Plutarch noted that ancient Egyptians burned galangal as a fumigant. Greek and Roman physicians included it in expensive remedies for wealthy patients, having acquired it via early Asian trade routes. In the Middle Ages, German herbalist Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) called galangal "the spice of life," though evidence suggests she was likely describing a different, related variety now known as lesser galangal — the type that became popular in European medieval and Tudor cookery, appearing in the 14th-century English cookbook *The Forme of Cury*. Today galangal is a key spice in Southeast Asian cuisine.

## Flavour Chemistry
Cineole and camphor drive galangal's penetrating, lingering pungency; unusual galangal acetate gives it a wasabi-like tang. Methyl cinnamate and fenchyl acetate contribute balsamic, cherry, and minty herbal notes.

## Pairing Logic
- Bay and cardamom enhance the eucalyptus qualities.
- Coriander offers a cooling camphorous match.
- Cinnamon and clove pair with the sweet methyl cinnamate side.
- Dill and caraway complement the rare fenchyl acetate compound.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Galangal's flavour has been likened to a mix of cardamom, ginger, and saffron, with mustard and citrus notes — it has a reputation for heightening other flavours while keeping its own character. Its mouth-numbing coolness comes from cineole directly stimulating a cold-sensing receptor on the tongue (TRPM8). Fresh galangal is best grated or pounded into paste; if substituting ground for fresh, use about half the amount.
