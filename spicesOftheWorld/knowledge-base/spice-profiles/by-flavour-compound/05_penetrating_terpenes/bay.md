# Bay

**Botanical name:** Laurus nobilis
**Taste profile:** Resinous, herbal, floral
**Also known as:** Sweet bay, bay laurel
**Flavour group:** Penetrating Terpenes

## Origin & History
Ancient Greeks and Romans regarded bay as symbolic of victory and high status; Romans used the leaves to flavour roast meats and believed garlands of bay could dispel evil spirits. The bay laurel spread from Asia Minor across the Mediterranean in ancient times and reached all of Europe by the Middle Ages, retaining spiritual significance — 17th-century English herbalist Nicholas Culpeper wrote that a bay tree would protect against harm, and branches were burned in public spaces during plague outbreaks.

## Flavour Chemistry
Cineole dominates with an unusually penetrating eucalyptus scent. Eugenol, the next most abundant compound, adds spicy, sweet, warming phenol character. Smaller amounts of phellandrene (minty, citrusy), pinene, terpineol, geraniol, and linalool round out the profile.

## Pairing Logic
- Black cardamom, cardamom, and galangal reinforce the cineole backbone.
- Clove, cinnamon, liquorice, and rosewater boost the eugenol-driven sweetness.
- Dill and black pepper complement the piquant phellandrene notes.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Fresh or dried leaves yield fragrant, warm flavours gradually when added at the start of cooking — traditionally part of a bouquet garni alongside thyme and parsley. Flavour compounds dissolve well in oil, fat, and alcohol but poorly in water. A good ratio for infusing is roughly one small leaf to three tablespoons of oil.

## Blends Featuring Bay
Burmese garam masala, Nanjing spice bag, Mulling spice, Khmeli-suneli
