# Barberry

**Botanical name:** Berberis vulgaris
**Taste profile:** Sour, sharp, tangy
**Also known as:** Piperidge, jaundice berry, zereshk
**Flavour group:** Sweet-Sour Acids / Fruity Aldehydes

## Origin & History
The earliest record of barberry use dates to 650 BCE, when its blood-purifying properties were inscribed on clay tablets in the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal. Chinese medicine has used it for over 3,000 years, and European medicinal use has nearly as long a history — the bark was historically used as a purgative and tonic. Barberry was widely used in preserves, syrups, and wines throughout the Middle Ages in western Europe. It was first cultivated in Iran over 200 years ago and has since become an important part of Middle Eastern and Caucasus cooking.

## Flavour Chemistry
Malic, tartaric, and citric acids give barberry its sharp, sour, lemony character. Hexanal and nonanal (green, grassy, fruity aldehydes) add complexity, while natural sugars (dextrose, fructose) provide sweetness.

## Pairing Logic
- Sumac, tamarind, and galangal share acid-driven sourness.
- Bay and ajwain enhance the green aldehyde qualities.
- Cacao, cinnamon, vanilla, and annatto balance sweetness against barberry's sourness and bitterness.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Versatile and tolerant of strong flavour pairings, with a jewel-like appearance that also makes it decorative. Rehydrating in cold water for 10 minutes (or a brief simmer) dilutes sharpness and speeds flavour release. Barberries are notably high in pectin (2.2%) — higher than strawberries, apricots, or unripe apples — making them useful for helping jams and jellies set quickly.
