# Carob

**Botanical name:** Ceratonia siliqua
**Taste profile:** Sweet, astringent, chocolatey
**Also known as:** St John's bread, locust bean, locust seed
**Flavour group:** Sweet-Sour Acids

## Origin & History
Carob has nourished humans and animals since ancient times, thanks to the tree's ability to fruit even in poor soil — a trait that made it vital during famines, and earned it a mention in the Bible as livestock fodder. The common names "St John's bread" and "locust bean" stem from the "locusts" St John the Baptist reportedly ate in the desert, which scholars believed were actually carob pods (though most now think he ate grasshoppers). The word "carat" derives from the Greek word for the carob tree, *keration*, since Arab jewellers once used carob seeds as a unit of weight for gold. Arabs carried carob west, and Greeks took it further; it reached Spain and Portugal by the 17th century and later spread to the New World.

## Flavour Chemistry
Pineapple ketone and pyruvic acid give a sweet, fruity, brown-sugar-caramel character. Pentanoic and hexanoic acids contribute sour milk and "sweaty cheese" notes; cinnamaldehyde adds warm, sweet, fragrant depth; pyrazines and farnesene contribute nutty, roasted, woody character on roasting.

## Pairing Logic
- Fenugreek, fennel, cardamom, and liquorice combine with the sugary fruit notes.
- Tamarind, sumac, barberry, and amchoor bring acidic tartness for balance.
- Cinnamon and cassia highlight the unroasted carob's cinnamaldehyde notes.
- Roasted sesame, cacao, wattle, grains of Selim, and nutmeg pair with the deep roasted flavours once carob itself is roasted.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Rich, warm, almost vanilla-like flavour resembling milk chocolate but without cacao's bitterness — a good sugar source in its own right. Roasting causes amino acids and sugars to react, creating nutty, coffee-and-chocolate-like compounds but reducing sweetness. Whole pods suit slow-cook dishes; for quick-cook dishes, grind pods or soak until soft.

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Cholesterol:** carob pulp has reduced total and LDL cholesterol in clinical research over as little as 6 weeks.
- **Blood sugar:** carob is a rich source of D-pinitol, a compound linked to improved insulin sensitivity.
- **Digestive:** its fibre bulks up digestion and has even shown promise reducing acute diarrhoea duration in children when paired with oral rehydration.
- **Weight management:** fibre content supports fullness, useful for appetite control.
- A genuinely useful "chocolate without the caffeine/bitterness" health angle for the episode framing.
