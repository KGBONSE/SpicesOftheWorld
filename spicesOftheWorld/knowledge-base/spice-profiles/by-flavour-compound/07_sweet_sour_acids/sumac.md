# Sumac

**Botanical name:** Rhus coriaria
**Taste profile:** Sour, fruity, woody
**Also known as:** Elm-leaved sumac
**Flavour group:** Sweet-Sour Acids

## Origin & History
The name "sumac" derives from the Aramaic word *summaq*, meaning "red." Long before lemons reached Europe, Romans imported sumac from Syria as both a souring agent and a dye; Pliny the Elder celebrated its astringent, cooling properties. Sumac has featured in Middle Eastern cooking since at least the 13th century CE as part of the za'atar spice blend, alongside sesame and dried herbs. Until the 1980s it was almost impossible to find outside the Middle East, but a wave of food writers championing Middle Eastern cuisine has since brought it a renaissance among Western cooks.

## Flavour Chemistry
Tannins and organic acids (mainly citric, tartaric, and malic) give sumac its sharp, mouth-puckering tartness. Caryophyllene contributes a musty, woody aroma; pinene adds fresh pine notes; "green" aldehydes like decanal and nonanal complement the freshness.

## Pairing Logic
- Coriander and nutmeg balance the sour acids with sweetness and warmth.
- Cardamom and vanilla pair with the green, fruity, floral aldehyde notes.
- Juniper complements the pinene-driven freshness.
- Cumin and fresh ginger enhance the woody, spicy warmth via caryophyllene.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Sharp and acidic, reminiscent of lemon balm, with a spicy earthy woody aroma — it can substitute anywhere lemon zest or juice would be used, or as a seasoning like salt. Sprinkle as a garnish at the end of cooking for maximum tartness and aroma impact, rather than adding early.
