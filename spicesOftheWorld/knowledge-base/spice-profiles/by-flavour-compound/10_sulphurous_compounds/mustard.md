# Mustard

**Botanical names:** Brassica alba (white), B. juncea (brown), B. nigra (black)
**Taste profile:** Pungent, earthy, sharp
**Also known as:** Indian mustard (brown)
**Flavour group:** Sulphurous Compounds

## Origin & History
Mustard seeds have been found at prehistoric sites from China to Europe. The first records of mustard as a condiment date back to the ancient Greeks and Romans, who chewed, pulverized, or steeped the seeds in wine. By the Middle Ages, white mustard had reached India and China via Arab trade routes, while brown mustard had travelled overland from its native India to Europe. One historical method of transporting mustard powder involved mixing it with flour and other spices and forming it into balls with honey, wine, or vinegar. The name "mustard" is thought to derive from the Latin *mustum*, the young wine once used to mix ground seeds into a paste.

## Flavour Chemistry
Isothiocyanates — sulphur-containing compounds — produce mustard's heat, uniquely vaporizing at body temperature for a nose-filling pungency. Pinene adds woody, pine notes; various compounds contribute coffee, peach, and chocolate-like character; toasting produces nutty, roasted pyrazine flavours.

## Pairing Logic
- Chilli, garlic, and ginger round out the hot, penetrating pungency.
- Cumin and bay add woodiness via pinene.
- Nigella and sesame pair with the roasted, nutty pyrazine notes.
- Cacao complements mustard's complex flavour remarkably well in both sweet and savoury dishes.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
White mustard seeds are mild and used in pickling and American yellow mustard; brown seeds are much hotter, common in Indian cuisine; black seeds are less common but finely aromatic. Whole seeds are bland until damaged: crushing or cooking activates the enzyme myrosinase, which reacts with sinalbin (white) or sinigrin (black/brown) to generate heat — but only in the presence of water. Toasting develops nutty, roasted flavours; soaking cracked seeds in water before cooking maximizes both flavour and heat.

## Blends Featuring Mustard
Panch phoran, Vindaloo paste

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Anti-inflammatory/heart:** glucosinolates in mustard seed show antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory action, linked to lower cardiovascular risk; its omega-3 content supports heart and neurological health.
- **Blood sugar:** mustard seed extract has shown meaningful anti-diabetic effects, improving insulin sensitivity in research.
- **Skin:** studied as a possible anti-inflammatory aid for skin conditions like psoriasis and contact dermatitis (animal research).
- **Cooking safety bonus:** using mustard as a marinade/coating for grilled meat has been shown to reduce formation of harmful compounds (HCAs/PAHs) from charring — genuinely useful for a grilling-focused channel.
- Most human-relevant evidence is preliminary — the marinade/HCA-reduction finding is the most directly useful one for on-camera cooking tips.
