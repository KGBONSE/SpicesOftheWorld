# Asafoetida

**Botanical name:** Ferula assa-foetida
**Taste profile:** Sulphurous, oniony, garlic-like
**Also known as:** Devil's dung, stinking gum, hing
**Flavour group:** Sulphurous Compounds

## Origin & History
Asafoetida was discovered in Persia in the 4th century BCE by soldiers of Alexander the Great, who mistook it for silphium — a widely used ancient spice from a similar, now-extinct plant. They carried it to Asia and the Mediterranean, where it became popular with Greeks and Romans as a silphium substitute, prized both as a seasoning and for its health-giving properties; it's cited throughout *Apicius*, a 1st-century Roman cookbook. After the fall of Rome it never regained popularity in Europe, though its use resurfaces in the *Baghdad Cookery Book* (1226). In the 16th century, the Mughals reportedly brought the spice to India, where it became indispensable to vegetarian and Ayurvedic cuisine across the subcontinent.

## Flavour Chemistry
Sulphides dominate the flavour-carrying oils, giving asafoetida its fried-onion smell and making it a natural partner for similarly flavoured spices like garlic. Phellandrene adds peppery, minty, turpentine-like character; acids and ocimene contribute sour, citrus, floral notes.

## Pairing Logic
- Garlic, mustard, and nigella share sulphur-driven onion-like notes.
- Barberry and coriander draw out underlying acidity.
- Peppercorn, cumin, grains of Selim, and bay enhance the peppery piquancy.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
When heated in fat, asafoetida develops a flavour similar to fried onions and garlic; traces of the same sulphurous compounds found in meat mean it can bring a meaty depth to vegetarian cooking. Its oil-based sulphur compounds dissolve very slowly in water — a useful property for controlling strength, e.g. tying a resin "tear" in muslin above a simmering pot, or steeping a piece in water for a milder flavoured liquid.
