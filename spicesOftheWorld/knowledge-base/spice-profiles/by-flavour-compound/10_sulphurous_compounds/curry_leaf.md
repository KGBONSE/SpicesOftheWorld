# Curry Leaf

**Botanical name:** Murraya koenigii
**Taste profile:** Meaty, warm, floral
**Also known as:** Karapincha, meetha neem, kari patta (not to be confused with the inedible "curry plant")
**Flavour group:** Sulphurous Compounds

## Origin & History
Curry leaf holds a special place in the cuisines of southern India, Sri Lanka, and Malaysia. When the Dravidians of northwest India moved south around 1000 BCE, they brought rice, mustard seeds, and pulses to plant, combining them with the indigenous curry leaves already growing there. Its use as a vegetable flavouring is documented in early Tamil literature from the 1st century CE. The botanical name *Murraya koenigii* actually honours two 18th-century botanists, Johann Andreas Murray and Johann Gerhard König, rather than referring to the plant's "kingly" aromatic qualities. It's grown prolifically in southern India as both an ornamental and kitchen garden plant.

## Flavour Chemistry
1-phenylethanethiol dominates, giving a meaty taste with hyacinth aroma and a sulphurous bite. Smaller amounts of floral linalool, pine-scented pinene, and penetrating cineole add complexity, while "green" hexanal and limonene provide citrus notes, especially in fresh leaves.

## Pairing Logic
- Asafoetida, garlic, mustard, and chilli combine with the hot, sulphurous notes.
- Nutmeg and black pepper pair with pinene for woody depth.
- Coriander and lemongrass foreground the floral linalool.
- Grains of paradise, cardamom, and black cardamom pair with the penetrating cineole side.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Fresh curry leaves have a mild, citrusy fragrance that turns musky and floral when bruised or sliced — quite unlike commercial curry powder. Flavour is subtle, so leaves can be used liberally. Dried leaves have very little flavour and are best avoided.
