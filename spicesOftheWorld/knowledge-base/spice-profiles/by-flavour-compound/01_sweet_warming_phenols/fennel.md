# Fennel

**Botanical name:** Foeniculum vulgare (bitter fennel), F. v. var. dulce (sweet fennel)
**Taste profile:** Aniseedy, warming, bittersweet
**Also known as:** Sweet cumin
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

## Origin & History
Fennel's Mediterranean roots run deep into classical history — the Greek word for the plant, *maratho*, gave its name to the plains of Marathon, site of the famous 490 BCE battle against the Persians. The Romans spread fennel cultivation across their empire and used it as a meat preservative, while Charlemagne is said to have mandated its cultivation on imperial farms in the early medieval period. European colonists later carried it to the Americas and Australia, where it now grows wild in places. Despite its Mediterranean identity, India is actually the largest modern producer, followed by Turkey, Japan, Argentina, and parts of North Africa.

## Flavour Chemistry
The dominant compound is anethole, giving fennel its characteristic sweet, herby, aniseed character. Fenchone contributes a bitter, camphor-like pungency, while smaller amounts of limonene (citrus/herby) and pinene (woody/pine) round out the profile.

## Pairing Logic
- Spices rich in similar liquorice-like compounds (anise, star anise, dill) reinforce fennel's sweet, herby side.
- Cardamom and black pepper draw out the woody, pine-like pinene notes.
- Cassia's camphor-like quality highlights fennel's more pungent, bitter fenchone side.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Fennel seeds are notable for flavouring Italian salami and feature throughout South Asian masala blends, from Kashmir to Sri Lanka. Grinding releases the oils held in canals just beneath the seed's ridges; dry-frying beforehand develops new roasted, nutty flavour compounds.
