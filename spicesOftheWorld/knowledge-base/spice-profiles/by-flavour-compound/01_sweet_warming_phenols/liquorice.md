# Liquorice

**Botanical name:** Glycyrrhiza glabra
**Taste profile:** Sweet, aniseedy, warm
**Also known as:** Sweetwood, Spanish juice plant, jethimadh
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

## Origin & History
Liquorice root has been chewed since antiquity by Assyrians, Babylonians, Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans as a thirst-quencher and stimulant. It travelled the Silk Road into China over 2,000 years ago, where it became valued as a poison antidote. By the 12th century, European monks were cultivating and prescribing liquorice extract for coughs and ulcers. The first liquorice sweet dates to 1760, and by the late 19th century sweets and liqueurs made with it were common across Europe, particularly in Nordic countries. Today its use as a genuine culinary spice remains largely confined to China, India, and Scandinavia — most of the global crop is grown for tobacco flavouring instead.

## Flavour Chemistry
Glycyrrhizin — around 50 times sweeter than sugar — drives liquorice's powerful sweetening effect. Estragole brings an aniseed, woody warmth; cineole adds a eucalyptus-like penetration; and eugenol contributes clove-like warming notes. Minor compounds add floral, cucumber, and oregano-like nuances.

## Pairing Logic
- Other warming, aniseed-forward spices (anise, fennel, star anise) reinforce liquorice's core character.
- Bay and cardamom intensify the eucalyptus-like cineole side.
- Clove and cinnamon pair with the eugenol-rich warming notes.
- Coriander and vanilla connect through liquorice's minor floral/creamy compounds.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
The root's intense sweetness and lingering warmth mean it should be used sparingly, added early in cooking to let its flavour diffuse — or steeped in hot water first for short-cook dishes. Its natural glycyrrhizin acts as a mild emulsifier, helping oil and water combine within a dish.
