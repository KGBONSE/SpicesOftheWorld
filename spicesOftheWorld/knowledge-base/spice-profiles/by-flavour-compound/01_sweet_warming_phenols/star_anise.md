# Star Anise

**Botanical name:** Illicium verum
**Taste profile:** Sweet, liquorice-like, warming, faintly bitter
**Also known as:** Chinese star anise, badian, bajiao, badiane
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

## Origin & History
Star anise is the dried fruit of a small evergreen tree native to southern
China and northern Vietnam, where it has been used in cooking and hot
drinks for well over a thousand years. It travelled west along the trade
routes from the 16th century, and by the 17th century was expensive enough
in Europe to be sold through apothecaries as a luxury. China is still by
far the dominant producer — the provinces of Guangxi and Yunnan account
for the large majority of world supply — and the trees take four to six
years to begin fruiting. It shares its defining flavour compound and its
name with aniseed and fennel but is not botanically related to either.

**Safety note worth knowing:** culinary star anise (*Illicium verum*) is
safe, but its close relative Japanese star anise (*Illicium anisatum*) is
toxic and looks similar — a reason whole pods from a reputable source are
safer than loose or unlabelled ground product, and a fair thing to
mention on camera if star anise tea comes up.

## Flavour Chemistry
Anethole dominates completely — it can make up 80–90% of the essential
oil, and it is the same compound that gives aniseed and fennel their
sweet, liquorice character. Smaller amounts of linalool (floral),
limonene (citrus), and foeniculin fill out the profile. Star anise is
also the main commercial source of shikimic acid, an unrelated compound
harvested from the pods as a starting material for the antiviral drug
oseltamivir (Tamiflu).

## Pairing Logic
- Aniseed, fennel, and liquorice reinforce the shared anethole sweetness.
- Cassia, cloves, and Sichuan pepper balance it with warmth and pungency — the classic five-spice logic.
- Citrus peel and ginger lift the heavier liquorice note.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
A cornerstone of Chinese cooking — one of the five spices in five-spice
powder, and a standard in red-cooked and master-stock braises, pho broth,
and tea eggs. The pods are usually simmered whole and fished out, or
lightly toasted and ground for a blend. A little goes a long way; one or
two pods is plenty for a pot. The flavour is fat- and alcohol-soluble and
holds up well to long cooking.

## Blends Featuring Star Anise
Five-spice powder, Nanjing spice bag, Shandong spice bag, Vindaloo paste

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-09-05)*
- **Antiviral raw material:** star anise is the industrial source of shikimic acid, the starting compound for Tamiflu — a genuine, well-known fact, though eating the spice is not the same as taking the drug, and that distinction matters on camera.
- **Antimicrobial:** anethole, shikimic acid, and linalool together show real antibacterial and antifungal activity in lab studies, including against some bacteria behind urinary and respiratory infections.
- **Antioxidant / anti-inflammatory:** the anethole-rich oil scavenges free radicals and shows anti-inflammatory effects in lab and animal work.
- Most of the evidence is lab- or animal-based — treat spoken claims as "this spice family" / "research shows" rather than proven human benefit.
