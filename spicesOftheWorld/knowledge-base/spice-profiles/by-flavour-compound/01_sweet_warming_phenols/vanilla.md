# Vanilla

**Botanical name:** Vanilla planifolia
**Taste profile:** Sweet, musky, woody
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

## Origin & History
Vanilla was first cultivated roughly 1,000 years ago on Mexico's eastern coast by the Totonac people, prized initially for its scent. The Aztecs later used it to flavour cacao drinks, and when Cortés was served such a drink by the Aztec king in 1519, he carried vanilla back to Spain along with cacao beans. Mexico held a monopoly on vanilla for centuries because the plant's flowers require pollination by a specific native orchid bee — until the 1830s, when a Belgian botanist worked out a hand-pollination technique that opened cultivation to the wider tropics. By 1874, German chemists had synthesized vanillin, and today 97% of vanilla-flavoured foods actually use the synthetic version rather than real vanilla.

## Flavour Chemistry
Vanillin makes up about 85% of vanilla's flavour, but more than 250 additional compounds — woody, spicy, floral, and fruity — are what separate real vanilla pods from synthetic vanillin. Guaiacol brings smoky, woody notes; anisaldehyde adds sweet, aniseed-like character; piperonal contributes a subtle floral cherry note.

## Pairing Logic
- Cinnamon and cassia intensify vanilla's natural sweetness and add spicy heat.
- Ginger and galangal bring complementary heat and cherry-like nuances.
- Liquorice is an ideal partner, sharing sweet glycyrrhizin and aniseed character.
- Clove, nutmeg, and anise connect through vanilla's minor aniseed-adjacent compounds.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Historically confined to sweet cookery, vanilla is increasingly used in savoury dishes, especially fish and seafood — though it can easily overpower a dish if overused. Many of vanilla's trace compounds evaporate quickly, so seeds should be used immediately and cooked over low heat to preserve subtlety. Vanilla varies significantly by origin: Madagascar/Réunion vanilla is rich and balanced; Mexican vanilla carries wine-like, fruity notes; Tahitian vanilla (the most expensive) has cherry-chocolate and liquorice notes; South Asian vanilla has lower vanillin content and a gentler, smokier flavour.

## Health Benefits *(not from the book — external research, added 2026-07-21)*
- **Antioxidant activity:** vanillin has shown strong free-radical-scavenging ability in lab studies, helping limit lipid damage to cells.
- **Anti-inflammatory effects:** vanilla compounds have reduced inflammation markers in laboratory research.
- **Mood/relaxation:** animal studies suggest vanillin's scent alone can ease anxiety-like behaviour, and small clinical studies found it can help soothe distressed infants.
- Evidence is genuinely early-stage — mostly animal/lab studies, not human trials, so keep any spoken claim soft.
