# Caraway

**Botanical name:** Carum carvi
**Taste profile:** Menthol, warming, earthy
**Also known as:** Carvies; incorrectly called wild cumin, Persian cumin, meridian fennel
**Flavour group:** Warming Terpenes

## Origin & History
Archaeologists have found caraway seeds in Stone Age refuse pits and 5,000-year-old Swiss dwellings, with the first written reference appearing around 1,500 BCE in an Egyptian herbal text. Egyptians placed the seeds in tombs to ward off evil spirits, and Romans knew it as *karo* or *careum*, spreading it to northern Europe. By the Middle Ages it was common in game, meat, bean, and cabbage cookery, plus sweeter confections and the German liqueur Kümmel (the German word for caraway). Folklore held that caraway seeds in a lover's pocket would keep them faithful.

## Flavour Chemistry
S-carvone, the dominant compound, gives caraway an unusually spicy flavour for a terpene, with hints of menthol and rye reminiscent of anise. Limonene adds citrus notes, and smaller amounts of sabinene contribute woody character. Notably, S-carvone is a mirror-image molecule of D-carvone — the compound responsible for spearmint's cooling flavour — meaning caraway's flavour is essentially spearmint "turned inside out."

## Pairing Logic
- Anise, star anise, cinnamon, and allspice deepen the liquorice/woody warmth.
- Cardamom and black pepper strengthen the citrus, zesty edge.
- Nutmeg and mace enhance the sweet citrus notes via shared sabinene.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
A key component of central European dishes, and of the North African blends tabil and harissa. Dry frying intensifies caraway's flavour but risks bitterness if over-toasted; cook with oil to help flavour compounds dissolve, and add late in cooking if using ground caraway.
