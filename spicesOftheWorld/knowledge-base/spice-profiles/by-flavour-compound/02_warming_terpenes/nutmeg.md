# Nutmeg

**Botanical name:** Myristica fragrans
**Taste profile:** Bittersweet, woody, warm
**Also known as:** True nutmeg, fragrant nutmeg
**Flavour group:** Warming Terpenes

## Origin & History
Nutmeg's home is the Banda Islands in Indonesia's Maluku (Moluccas) archipelago, and its history is notably turbulent. Traders kept its source secret to protect their monopoly, and by the 16th century European demand made it more valuable than gold, sparking a race for control. Portugal took the Moluccas in 1511, the Dutch seized control in 1599, and England took two of the Banda Islands in 1603. The Dutch valued nutmeg so highly that they traded one of the islands back to England in exchange for a New World settlement then called New Amsterdam — today's New York City.

## Flavour Chemistry
Myristicin gives nutmeg its signature woody aroma, despite being only a small proportion of the oil. Eugenol and cineole add clove-like, eucalyptus-penetrating notes; geraniol and safrole bring rosy sweetness; sabinene contributes orangey, peppery, woody character. Nutmeg also contains neolignans — compounds recently discovered to trigger a numbing, cooling sensation on the tongue by acting on cold-temperature receptors, despite nutmeg not tasting cold.

## Pairing Logic
- Cardamom, grains of Selim, and cassia share nutmeg's penetrating eugenol/cineole profile.
- Allspice and curry leaf complement the sweet, geraniol-driven side.
- Ginger and black pepper pair with the warming myristicin backbone.
- Garlic and juniper bring out sabinene's fruity piquancy.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Nutmeg's flavour oils are among the most volatile of any spice — pre-ground nutmeg loses potency fast, so grating fresh is strongly preferred. A little goes a long way ("a grating literally means one stroke on a grater"). Best added towards the end of cooking since the oils evaporate quickly.

## Blends Featuring Nutmeg
Turkish baharat, Bumbu, Jamaican jerk rub, Mulling spice, Quatre épices
