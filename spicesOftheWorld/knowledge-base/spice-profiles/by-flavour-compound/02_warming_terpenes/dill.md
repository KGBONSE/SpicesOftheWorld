# Dill

**Botanical name:** Anethum graveolens
**Taste profile:** Bitter, citrus, woody
**Also known as:** False aniseed
**Flavour group:** Warming Terpenes

## Origin & History
Dill seeds and fronds were used medicinally in Egypt as early as 3000 BCE, and the ancient Greeks valued its digestive and sedative properties. It became a seasoning for vinegars and pickles in Scandinavia and central/eastern Europe by the Middle Ages, and was also associated with witchcraft, love potions, and aphrodisiac use. Kosher dill pickles became a dietary staple for Jewish communities in eastern Europe and were introduced to the US by immigrants in the late 1800s/early 1900s, making dill an important American pickling crop — though today most of that seed supply actually comes from India.

## Flavour Chemistry
D-carvone dominates dill's flavour profile, giving it a spicy character with menthol and rye-like liquorice notes. Citrus-scented limonene is the other major compound, alongside small amounts of bitter fenchone and minty phellandrene.

## Pairing Logic
- Caraway, anise, and fennel echo dill's liquorice/herby character.
- Coriander, cardamom, and ginger boost the citrus limonene notes.
- Allspice, star anise, and bay bring out the minty, lemony phellandrene side.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Dill's flavour resembles a cross between anise and a milder caraway. Toasting creates new nutty flavour compounds; both new and existing compounds dissolve poorly in water, so cooking in oil or fat helps release the flavour. Dill's compounds also dissolve well in alcohol, which is part of why fermentation-based pickling works so effectively for dill pickles.
