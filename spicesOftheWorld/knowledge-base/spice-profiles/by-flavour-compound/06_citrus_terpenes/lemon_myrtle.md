# Lemon Myrtle

**Botanical name:** Backhousia citriodora
**Taste profile:** Citrusy, warm, bitter
**Also known as:** Sweet verbena tree, lemon ironwood
**Flavour group:** Citrus Terpenes

## Origin & History
The potent lemony foliage of the lemon myrtle tree has been prized by Aboriginal Australians for possibly millennia, both in cooking and to treat cuts. First distilled for essential oil in 1888, an extract was used as a substitute for commercial lemon essence during World War II when supplies grew scarce. It wasn't cultivated commercially as a spice until the early 1990s, and demand for it is now outstripping supply, with plantations established outside Australia. Since 2010, the species has faced a threat from myrtle rust, a fungal infection.

## Flavour Chemistry
Citral makes up almost the entirety of lemon myrtle's leaf oil — up to 30 times more concentrated than in lemon rind itself. Smaller amounts of floral linalool, peppery myrcene, and the green-tasting ketone sulcatone round out the profile.

## Pairing Logic
- Lemongrass, wattle, cacao, and chilli pair with or complement the lemon component.
- Cinnamon and ginger reinforce the floral linalool qualities.
- Bay complements the green sulcatone-driven freshness.
- Sichuan pepper and black pepper harmonize with the peppery myrcene side.

## Culinary Uses & Technique
Brighter and more lemony than actual lemon, but without the acidity — meaning lemon myrtle can be added to milk- or cream-based dishes without curdling risk, unlike acidic citrus. Its citral compound is unstable and breaks down over time, letting camphor and eucalyptus notes dominate, so it's best added towards the end of cooking or used in short-cook recipes with some fat present.
