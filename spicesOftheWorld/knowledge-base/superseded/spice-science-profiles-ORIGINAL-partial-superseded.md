## CINNAMON — "Sweet, Aromatic, Warm"

**Botanical name:** *Cinnamomum verum*
**Also known as:** Ceylon cinnamon, "true" cinnamon
**Major flavour compound:** Cinnamaldehyde
**Parts used:** Dried bark of tender shoots
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

**The plant:** a small evergreen tree in the laurel family, found wild in wet tropical forests. Young shoots are harvested for their bark every two years.

**Cultivation & preparation:** trees are coppiced at 18–24 months old and the stump covered, causing it to grow like a bush; new shoots are removed at the base and stripped for their bark. The inner layers of bark are dried in the sun and rolled by hand into long "quills," then graded and cut.

**Buying/storage tips:** ground cinnamon loses its flavour quickly — buy in small quantities, keep airtight in a cool dark place, and use within 6 months. Cinnamon sticks keep their flavour for up to a year; lighter brown, thinner, more fragile sticks are the higher-quality ones.

**Non-culinary uses:** perfumery, and as a natural antiseptic.

**Spice story:** from 1600 BCE, ancient Egyptians used a type of cinnamon for incense and embalming, importing it from Asia via African traders — it's not known for certain whether this was true Sri Lankan cinnamon or Chinese cassia. From the 8th century CE, Arab merchants dominated the trade and invented tall tales to protect their sources and keep prices high — one myth claimed giant birds gathered the bark to build nests on high cliffs, and the only way to collect it was to lure the birds away with large pieces of meat. The real source stayed a mystery to Europeans until the Portuguese found cinnamon trees growing in Sri Lanka in the early 1500s and promptly occupied the island. They were in turn ousted by the Dutch, who fought the British for centuries over control of the territory and its lucrative trade.

**Region of cultivation:** native to Sri Lanka; now also cultivated in Myanmar, Vietnam, Indonesia, and the Seychelles (off the coast of East Africa — a direct link back to your Africa file).

### Blending science
Cinnamaldehyde, the dominant compound, is sensed by temperature receptors on the tongue — giving cinnamon its warming quality and making it a natural partner for other warming spices. Further pairing routes come through the woodiness of caryophyllene, the penetrating aroma of eugenol, and the floral notes of linalool.

| Compound | Character | Pairing route | Partner spices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Caryophyllene | Spicy, woody, dry | Enhance woody notes | Allspice (shares eugenol, warming spiciness); black peppercorn (pungent heat from piperine) |
| Cinnamaldehyde | Warming, spicy, pungent | Add depth with an earthier warming compound | Cumin (equally warming, tenacious cuminaldehyde) |
| Linalool | Floral, woody, spicy | Draw out the floral notes | Cardamom (complex citrus, floral, penetrating eucalyptus aromas) |
| Eugenol | Medicinal, woody, warming | Pair with other eugenols for clean, penetrating aromas | Clove (shares caryophyllene, use sparingly); anise and star anise (enhance the sweetening effect, add liquorice notes) |

**Food partners:**
- **Fruit** — mix cinnamon powder with sugar and scatter over peaches, figs, apples, and pears before baking or grilling, or add to the batter for a plum or cherry clafoutis.
- **Sweet bakes** — ground cinnamon flavours Nordic buns, Italian panforte, and French pain d'épices.
- **Tomatoes and aubergines** — a cinnamon-infused tomato sauce is an excellent topping for baked aubergines.
- **Red meats** — add a stick or two to a lamb tagine, an Iranian khoresh beef stew, or the stock of a Vietnamese beef pho.
- **Pigeon** — cinnamon is the main flavouring in Moroccan pastilla pigeon pie.

**Blends to try (cross-referenced to your regional files):** Advieh (Iran — Middle East file), Burmese garam masala (Southeast Asia file), Jamaican jerk rub (Caribbean — Americas file), Mole mix (Mexico — Americas file), Mulling spice (Great Britain — Europe file).

**Releasing the flavour:** cinnamaldehyde doesn't dissolve in water and needs time to escape cinnamon's woody structure. Add it early in cooking; fat and alcohol help disperse it; steam also carries it, so boil vigorously with a lid on the pan.

## CASSIA — "Sweet, Peppery, Astringent"

**Botanical name:** *Cinnamomum cassia*, *C. loureirii*, *C. burmannii*
**Also known as:** Chinese cinnamon; Vietnamese/Saigon cinnamon (*C. loureirii*); Indonesian Java/Korintje cinnamon (*C. burmannii*)
**Major flavour compound:** Cinnamaldehyde (same as true cinnamon)
**Parts used:** Dried bark, and unripe fruits ("buds")
**Flavour group:** Sweet Warming Phenols

**The plant:** an evergreen tree in the laurel family, closely related to cinnamon — but unlike "true" cinnamon, its leaves and buds are also aromatic. The bark itself is coarser and greyish-brown, darker, thicker, more loosely coiled, and harder to snap than true cinnamon — carrying a stronger, more intense flavour.

**Cultivation & preparation:** bark is harvested every second year in the monsoon season, from trees at least four years old. Strips of inner bark are dried in the sun and curl up naturally into thick shards.

**Non-culinary uses:** perfumes; in Chinese medicine to treat diarrhoea and dyspepsia.

**Spice story:** cassia was used medicinally in ancient China from 2700 BCE, and was among the first spices to reach the Mediterranean via the ancient trade routes. The Egyptians used cinnamon both culinarily and for its health properties, though it's unclear whether they meant cinnamon or cassia. Persians knew both as *darchini*, using them in savoury and sweet dishes. By the 5th century BCE, cassia had been identified as distinct from cinnamon. Medieval English and French cookbooks referred to both as "canella," but cassia's coarser flavour saw its status demoted — a 15th-century book of manners noted that the finer spice was "for lords," the coarser "for common people." Today cassia accounts for nearly 50% of the world's cinnamon supply and is a key spice across China and Southeast Asia; it's also what most "ground cinnamon" sold in North America actually is.

**Region of cultivation:** native to the wet tropical forests of southern China; cultivated across southern and eastern Asia, mainly southern China, Vietnam, and Indonesia.

### Blending science
Cassia shares cinnamaldehyde with true cinnamon, but also contains tannins (giving mouth-puckering astringency) and coumarin — a phenol absent from true cinnamon — plus eucalyptus-scented cineole.

| Compound | Character | Pairing route | Partner spices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cinnamaldehyde | Warming, sweet, cinnamon-like | Pair with earthy/bitter/gently warming spices | Carob (sweet, earthy, contains cinnamaldehyde itself); cumin (earthy, slightly bitter warmth); star anise (herbal, earthy, floral, undercurrent of liquorice); ginger (pungent warmth, sweet citrus notes) |
| Coumarin | Sweet, warming, grassy | Combine with other sweet spices for a broader profile | Mahleb (almond flavour); nutmeg and vanilla (bittersweet woodiness, honeyed sweetness); anise (intensifies sweetness, adds herby notes) |
| Cineole | Penetrating, eucalyptus, fresh | Pair with cineole-flavoured spices to enhance the eucalyptus nuance | Cardamom (sweet, minty background); bay (rich in cineole, adds complexity/piney/floral freshness); allspice (complex peppery warmth); grains of Selim (medicinal, pine, floral, woody notes) |

**Food partners:**
- **Beef, pork** — a piece of cassia bark alongside other warming spices in an Italian beef/oxtail ragu, beef rendang, or pork vindaloo.
- **Pulses, grains** — added to the aromatic base for a pilaf, dhal, or curry.
- **Baking** — the unmistakable aroma of American iced cinnamon rolls uses ground bark; ground buds go into Christmas confections, fruit cake, and spiced biscuit dough.
- **Preserves** — infuse cucumber pickling brine, tomato chutney, or barbecue sauce with cassia buds.

**Blend to try:** Garam masala (North India — South Asia file).

**⚠️ Coumarin caution (worth mentioning on camera as a genuine safety note):** sweet-tasting coumarin can cause temporary liver damage if consumed in excessive amounts. Regular consumers of cinnamon-flavoured foods should choose true cinnamon rather than cassia where possible. Recommended maximum weekly amounts: children 3.5g, adults 7g.

**Releasing the flavour:** grind cassia just before use to minimize flavour-oil loss through evaporation; include fat and/or alcohol to help disperse the compounds; a water-based dish can still pick up cinnamaldehyde if boiled vigorously with a lid on.

## CLOVE — "Sweet, Astringent, Camphorous"

**Botanical name:** *Syzygium aromaticum*
**Also known as:** "Nail spice" — many languages' names for clove translate this way, due to its shape.
**Major flavour compound:** Eugenol
**Parts used:** Flower bud
**Flavour group:** *(page not yet captured — likely Sweet Warming Phenols alongside cinnamon and cassia, given eugenol overlap)*

**The plant:** a tropical evergreen that flourishes in volcanic, loamy soil. Unpicked buds develop into crimson flowers with a creamy froth of stamens; the "round tops" seen on dried cloves are actually the unopened flower petals. The tree's glossy, bay-like leaves are also aromatic.

**Cultivation & preparation:** flower buds are picked by hand twice a year, just as they turn pinky-red and are almost ready to open; they're then dried in the sun until dark brown and hardened.

**Buying tips:** look for plump, unshrivelled, unbroken cloves that have retained their rounded tops; for quality, pressing the "stem" should release a little oil under fingernail pressure. Ground cloves lose flavour quickly — better to buy whole and grind as needed.

**Non-culinary uses:** flavouring kretek cigarettes in Indonesia; some dental products; traditionally used to treat nausea and indigestion.

**Spice story — a direct link back to your Southeast Asia file:** the Moluccas (now Maluku), Indonesia, were once renowned as the Spice Islands thanks to a trio of indigenous spices — cloves, nutmeg, and mace — cultivated there and nowhere else for almost two millennia. Courtiers addressing the Chinese emperor during the Han dynasty (206 BCE–220 CE) used cloves to sweeten their breath; the Romans, who named the spice *clavus* (Latin for nail), used it as incense and perfume. It took off as a culinary spice in the West during the Middle Ages. The Republic of Venice initially held a virtual monopoly on the trade, but the Portuguese, Dutch, Spanish, and English fought a series of wars to seize control, with the Dutch eventually winning out. In the 18th century, the same Frenchman from your Europe file — Pierre Poivre — smuggled clove seedlings out to Mauritius, breaking that Dutch monopoly for good.

**Region of cultivation:** Indonesia is the world's largest producer of cloves, though most of its own crop is used domestically by the kretek cigarette industry. *(Additional producer detail was cut off in this batch — worth capturing if you send the rest of this page.)*

## RECIPE EXAMPLE — Chinese Steamed Salmon with Chilli and Star Anise

*(This is a worked example from the book showing how a science-informed recipe actually reads — useful as a direct model for your own "classic dish + home recipe" segment.)*

**Serves 4 · Prep 30 mins · Cook 10 mins**

**Ingredients:** 4 lightly smoked salmon fillets (skinned); ½ tsp ground white pepper; a handful of star anise, broken up; 1 tbsp Shaoxing wine or dry sherry; 1 tbsp finely chopped fresh root ginger; 1 Scotch bonnet chilli, deseeded and cut into ~12 strips; 2 tsp light soy sauce; 2 tsp dark soy sauce; 3 finely chopped spring onions; 1 tbsp vegetable oil; 2 tsp sesame oil; 1 tsp chilli oil.

**Method:**
1. Rub the salmon fillets with white pepper. Arrange in a single layer in a heatproof dish, with one piece of star anise underneath and one on top of each fillet.
2. Pour in the wine or sherry, scatter the ginger over the fish, and place chilli strips on top of each fillet.
3. Add water to a depth of 2cm in a steamer or deep pan with a trivet; bring to a simmer. Place the fish dish in the pan, cover tightly with foil, and steam over low-medium heat for 8–10 minutes depending on thickness. Top up with hot water if needed.
4. Once cooked, remove the star anise pieces, pour the soy sauces over the fish, and scatter with spring onions.
5. Heat a wok or heavy pan over high heat, add the three oils, and swirl round the pan. When very hot and just smoking, remove from heat and pour over the fish. Serve with steamed rice and stir-fried greens such as pak choi.

**Spice ideas for variation (a good model for your own "how to remix this" segment):**
- Swap in different coloured peppercorns: black pepper for more aromatic, complex heat; green pepper for dominant herbaceous notes.
- Alternatives to star anise: allspice will pick out the fruity esters in the chilli; citrus-floral coriander will enhance the citral notes in the ginger.
- Replace fresh chillies with dried (rehydrated and sliced) for smokier, more toasted flavours.
