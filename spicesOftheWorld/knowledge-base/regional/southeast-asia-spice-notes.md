## Regional overview
When Portuguese traders brought the chilli pepper to Southeast Asia, they triggered a culinary revolution — chilli now dominates spice palettes across the entire region, alongside the characteristic fresh trio of garlic, ginger, and lemongrass.

**Key historic waypoints:**
- **The Moluccas** (now the Malukus), known historically as the **Spice Islands**, were for centuries the world's only source of cloves, nutmeg, and mace — making them a magnet for traders and invaders, first from China, India, and the Middle East, later from European nations.
- **Melaka (Malacca)** was, during the 15th century, a major trading port for Arab and Chinese merchants, thanks to its position on shipping routes through the Indo-Chinese seas.
- **"Spice wars":** the West's hunger for spices made this region and its sea routes hot property — between the 16th and 18th centuries there were repeated conflicts as the Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, and British all vied for control.
- **Manila galleons:** Spanish ships sailed between Manila and Acapulco (Mexico) trading spices and goods — a direct link between Southeast Asia and the Americas.
- **Jakarta:** the Dutch first landed here in 1596, and it quickly became the hub for Dutch spice traders in the region.
- **Singapore:** the British used it as their regional centre for spice trading and processing.
- Indonesia alone provides roughly one-fifth of the world's black pepper.

## 1. Myanmar — "Punchy, Fragrant, Savoury"

- **Signature spices:** ginger, garlic, chilli.
- **Supporting:** garam masala, mild curry powder, sesame, coriander, lemongrass, turmeric, cumin.
- **Supplementary:** black pepper, star anise, cinnamon, curry leaf, tamarind, poppy.
- **Flavour identity:** varied terrain, climate, and ethnicity (Farrimond notes influences from over 100 indigenous groups) bring eclectic flavours to the Burmese table. Dry spices from India and Bangladesh are used sparingly to enhance freshly pounded spice pastes; the cuisine is less sweet than neighbouring Thailand, with sour notes and a preference for subtle pungency over pure heat. Fresh, fragrant leaves — both cultivated and wild — find their way into most dishes, alongside fermented fish products.
- **Trade note:** centuries of trade across the Bay of Bengal integrated Indian flavours well into local recipes — garam masala is a particularly popular import.
- **Distinctive touch:** pandan leaves impart a vanilla-like flavour, especially in coconut-based sweet dishes.
- **Local blend recipe — Burmese Garam Masala:** dry-roast 1 tsp coriander seeds, 1 tsp black peppercorns, 1 tsp cumin seeds, 2 dried bay leaves, 1 tsp cardamom pods, 1 tsp cloves, a 2.5cm cinnamon stick, and 2 star anise until fragrant; cool, then grind to a fine powder. India's most famous spice mix, equally popular in Myanmar.

## 2. Thailand, Laos, and Cambodia — "Hot, Fresh, Clean"

- **Signature spices:** chilli, garlic, coriander root, lemongrass.
- **Supporting:** lime leaf, galangal, ginger, krachai (wild ginger), black pepper, tamarind.
- **Supplementary:** turmeric, clove, nutmeg, cinnamon, fennel, cardamom, pandan leaf, green pepper.
- **Flavour identity:** no country along the spice route embraced chilli as enthusiastically as Thailand — it's impossible to imagine Thai food without it, though the blander Cambodian kitchen hints at what pre-chilli Thai food might have tasted like. Like land-locked Laos, Thai chefs still favour freshly pounded spice pastes over dried spice mixes. Cambodian cuisine draws its subtler heat from black pepper, grown in the country since the 13th century.
- **Local blend recipe — Khao Kua:** dry-roast 5 tbsp uncooked glutinous ("sticky") rice with 1 sliced lemongrass stalk, a thumb-sized piece of sliced galangal, and 3 roughly torn makrut lime leaves in a frying pan over medium heat, stirring frequently, until the rice turns golden brown; cool, pick out the aromatics, then pound the rice with a pestle and mortar until coarsely ground. A key ingredient in Laotian larb and Thai beef salad — its texture can't be substituted.

## 3. Vietnam — "Fresh, Warm, Delicate"

- **Signature spices:** ginger, garlic, chilli.
- **Supporting:** lemongrass, coriander root, star anise, allspice, turmeric, dill, pandan leaf, black pepper.
- **Supplementary:** cardamom, liquorice, fennel, cinnamon, clove.
- **Flavour identity:** less influenced by the spice trade than Malaysia or Indonesia, but Vietnam still keeps a well-stocked spice cupboard. Most dishes carry vital base notes from dried aromatics, though it's the abundance of fresh herbs that stands out on the plate. Southern Vietnamese cooking is more richly spiced than the north; sour and salty notes run throughout the country. French colonialists later brought a delicate hand with more European spices — dill, fennel, and liquorice.
- **Trade note:** bird's eye chillies, popular throughout Vietnam, were most likely introduced in the 16th century by the Portuguese.
- **Local blend recipe — Nuoc Cham:** pound 2 chopped bird's eye chillies, 1 chopped garlic clove, and 2 tsp palm sugar to a paste in a pestle and mortar; squeeze in the juice of half a lime, then add 2 tbsp water and 2 tbsp Vietnamese fish sauce (nuoc mam); mix well. No Vietnamese table is complete without a bowl of this dipping sauce.

## 4. Malaysia and Singapore — "Complex, Rich, Fusion"

- **Signature spices:** ginger, garlic, chilli.
- **Supporting:** cardamom, turmeric, dried chilli, black pepper, coriander seed and root, cumin, cinnamon, curry leaf, lemongrass, galangal, tamarind, pandan leaf, lime leaf.
- **Supplementary:** fennel, anise, star anise, dried ginger, cassia, Sichuan pepper, sesame.
- **Flavour identity:** the southern Malay port of Melaka was ideally placed to become a major trading hub for both regional shipping and spice-route traffic bound further afield. As a result, significant Chinese, Indian, and Muslim communities endure in Malaysia and Singapore to this day, and the cuisine is defined by its willingness to embrace varied cultural influences. Fusion food, as a concept, was arguably born in Melaka — where Chinese, Indian, Arabic, and European ingredients regularly ended up in the same pot.
- **Local blend recipe — Malaysian Fish Curry Paste:** toast 2 tsp coriander seeds, 1 tsp cumin seeds, ½ tsp fennel seeds, ½ tsp black peppercorns, and 4 dried chillies; cool and grind with ½ tsp turmeric. Separately blend a thumb-sized piece of galangal, 3 garlic cloves, and 50g shallots with a splash of water into a purée. Combine the dry masala with the fresh purée to form the curry paste — then simmer with coconut milk and firm white fish.

## 5. Indonesia — "Vibrant, Complex, Diverse"

- **Signature spices:** garlic, ginger, shallot.
- **Supporting:** nutmeg, mace, clove, salam leaf, galangal, lemongrass, pandan leaf, lime leaf, tamarind, chilli, turmeric, cumin, cinnamon.
- **Supplementary:** black pepper, coriander, curry leaf, cardamom, curry powder, caraway, anise.
- **Flavour identity:** spread across an archipelago of more than 17,000 islands, Indonesia's spice palette is as eclectic as its people's heritage. The traditional saying "if your eyes do not water, the food is not good" reflects how thoroughly the nation embraced the 16th-century arrival of chilli — though there's far more to Indonesian food than heat alone. Indigenous nutmeg, mace, and cloves mingle with fresh aromatic leaves and imports from Persia, India, and China to create genuinely complex dishes.
- **Local blend recipe — Bumbu:** pound 1 tsp coriander seeds, 3 cloves, ½ tsp white peppercorns, and a pinch of grated nutmeg with 1.5cm galangal and 2.5cm fresh turmeric; add 6 chopped shallots, 3 chopped garlic cloves, and 2 chopped red chillies; finally mix in 1 tsp shrimp paste. Fry in 1 tbsp oil over high heat, stirring, for ~5 minutes until golden. Nearly every Indonesian recipe is built on some version of this base spice paste — every region, and every cook, has their own favourite.

## 6. Philippines — "Mild, Colourful, Aromatic"

- **Signature spices:** bay, black pepper, garlic — the "holy trinity" of Filipino cuisine, appearing in nearly every savoury recipe including the unofficial national dish, adobo.
- **Supporting:** annatto, turmeric, ginger, lemongrass, pandan leaf.
- **Supplementary:** chilli powder, paprika, tamarind.
- **Flavour identity:** despite how central food is to Filipino life (five meals a day is the norm), the spicing itself is surprisingly restrained — most classic recipes use no more than two or three spices, and very few would be classed as genuinely "spicy." Despite 300+ years of Spanish rule leaving the palate feeling more European than Asian, the Philippines never took to chilli the way Spain and its other trading partners did. Spices here are used more for colour (red from annatto, yellow from turmeric) than for heat.
- **Trade note:** annatto is a New World native, brought over and now grown locally in the Philippines. *Adobo* is itself a Spanish term applied by colonists to a native Filipino technique of preserving meat with vinegar and spices.
- **Local blend recipe — Adobo Marinade:** combine 100ml coconut vinegar, 50ml soy sauce, 3 crushed garlic cloves, 1 tsp black peppercorns, and 2 bay leaves in a small saucepan; bring to the boil, then remove from heat and cool to room temperature before using to marinate chicken or pork overnight.
