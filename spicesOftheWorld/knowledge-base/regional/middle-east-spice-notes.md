## Regional overview
The Middle East sits at the heart of the East–West spice trade going back millennia. The flavour palette shifts across the region: bright and green on the eastern Mediterranean coast, drier and more intense across the Arabian deserts, sweeter and fresher in Iran's fertile interior. From 700–1450 CE, global spice commerce was dominated by Arab merchants sailing the trade winds to bring goods from India and Southeast Asia — turmeric, black pepper, cloves, and nutmeg among them.

**Key historic waypoints:**
- **Istanbul** — its position straddling two continents made it a meeting point for European and Asian spice traders.
- **Alexandria (Egypt)** — the main hub of the ancient spice trade until power shifted to Rome.
- **Baghdad** — became the premier Silk Road city after 770 CE.
- **The "Road to China"** — an overland route active from 114 BCE, the conduit for East Asian spices like ginger and star anise into Turkish and Persian cuisine; rose petals were first brought to the region by Chinese traders along it.
- **Muscat (Oman)** — a key stopping point for spice ships travelling between the Indian Ocean and the Middle East.
- **Ancient Egyptians** founded the spice trade, importing goods from Africa and Arabia as early as 3000 BCE.

## 1. Syria — "Fruity, Warm, Sour"

- **Signature spices:** Aleppo pepper, sumac, allspice, black pepper, garlic.
- **Supporting:** safflower, cumin, cinnamon, cardamom, nigella, sesame.
- **Supplementary:** tamarind, nutmeg, caraway, anise.
- **Flavour identity:** often overlooked next to better-known neighbours Turkey and Lebanon, Syria's food culture is rich in fresh herbs, cherries, dates, pomegranates, and nuts, laced with a fragrant, mild spice palette. Syrians favour a milder spice profile than their Turkish or Lebanese neighbours — choosing safflower over saffron, for instance.
- **Star ingredient:** Aleppo pepper — a mild chilli flake produced around the ancient Silk Road town of Aleppo — is considered the standout of the Syrian spice rack.
- **Trade/history note:** *baharat* simply means "spices," and it's the blend that defines the Middle East as a whole — though its exact mix varies region to region.
- **Local blend recipe — Za'atar:** dry-roast 2 tbsp cumin seeds with 1 tsp sea salt until fragrant, grind; separately dry-roast 2 tbsp sesame seeds until golden; combine with 2 tbsp dried oregano and 2 tbsp sumac. Used on hummus, labneh, cheeses, meats, and fish.

## 2. Turkey — "Fresh, Mild, Smoky"

- **Signature spices:** pul biber (mild, coarsely ground chilli), black pepper, cumin, garlic, sumac.
- **Supporting:** urfa biber (a darker, smokier chilli), paprika, allspice, nigella, cinnamon.
- **Supplementary:** fenugreek, clove, nutmeg, coriander, cardamom — Asian flavours are notably common in Turkish cooking.
- **Flavour identity:** sitting at the crossroads of Europe, Asia, and the Middle East, Turkey has long been a spice-trading hub. Istanbul's domed Spice Bazaar historically held Iranian saffron and Sichuan pepper alongside homegrown pul biber and urfa biber. Aegean/Mediterranean coastal dishes lean on fresh herbs and lighter spicing; eastern Turkish dishes carry heavier spicing and sweetness, closer to a Middle Eastern flavour profile.
- **Local blend recipe — Turkish Baharat:** grind 2 tbsp black peppercorns, 2 tbsp cumin seeds, 2 tbsp coriander seeds, 1 tsp cloves, ½ tbsp cardamom seeds, 1 tbsp ground nutmeg, and a pinch of ground cinnamon; mix in 1 tbsp dried mint. Makes ~9 tbsp. Used in meat kofte, pilafs, and roast vegetables (especially aubergine).

## 3. Israel — "Varied, Earthy, Sweet"

- **Signature spices:** sesame, sumac, cumin, garlic, paprika.
- **Supporting:** caraway, chilli, fenugreek, nigella, black pepper, cinnamon, cardamom, coriander.
- **Supplementary:** turmeric, hibiscus, rose, saffron, bay.
- **Flavour identity:** shares much of its menu with neighbouring Lebanon, but draws its ever-evolving palette from across the globe — fiery chilli pastes from Yemen, paprika from Russia and Eastern Europe, harissa from North Africa, and (increasingly) Korean, Thai, and Mexican influences brought home by Israeli travellers. Zhug, a bright green chilli condiment, is a national staple served with falafel, grilled meats, and egg dishes.
- **Distinctive touch:** dried hibiscus flowers — steeped in water or ground to powder for a sharp, cranberry-like fruitiness — are uncommon elsewhere in the Middle East but a fixture of Israeli spicing. Caraway seasons Jewish rye bread; nigella seeds top pittas.

## 4. Lebanon — "Bright, Warm, Aniseedy"

- **Signature spices:** coriander, cinnamon, black pepper, allspice, clove, ginger, nutmeg (these seven make up the Lebanese *baharat*).
- **Supporting:** garlic, sumac, paprika, cumin, cardamom, sesame, fenugreek.
- **Supplementary:** saffron, turmeric, caraway, anise.
- **Flavour identity:** Lebanese mezze is the Middle East's most famous culinary export, though dishes like hummus are hotly disputed in origin between Israel and Lebanon. Lebanese cooks use spices liberally, with the seven-spice baharat a kitchen staple for mezzes, marinades, soups, and stews. The sweet-toothed Lebanese palate favours aniseed-spiced desserts like meghli rice pudding and sfouf tea cake.
- **Local blend recipe — Zhug** *(Israel's national condiment, also used widely in Lebanon)*: roast and grind 1 tsp each cardamom, coriander, and cumin seeds; pound to a paste with 2 chopped green chillies and 2 chopped garlic cloves; combine with a large bunch each of chopped coriander and flat-leaf parsley plus 2 tbsp olive oil; season with salt, pepper, and the juice of half a lemon.
- **Local blend recipe — Taklia:** gently fry 3 sliced garlic cloves in 2 tbsp olive oil until fragrant (not coloured); grind with 1 tsp ground coriander, a pinch of cayenne, and ½ tsp sea salt. Added to soups and stews just before serving.

## 5. Iraq — "Sweet, Fragrant, Sour"

- **Signature spices:** black pepper, cardamom, cinnamon, cumin.
- **Supporting:** allspice, garlic, turmeric, tamarind, saffron, dried lime.
- **Supplementary:** fenugreek, cassia, paprika, coriander, ginger.
- **Flavour identity:** spices have been part of Iraqi cooking since the rulers of ancient Mesopotamia. Persian traders added fresh, fruity spices; Silk Road/Spice Route traders layered in sweet-and-sour Chinese cassia alongside South Asia's cardamom, turmeric, and fenugreek. Black pepper is the preferred seasoning for Iraq's favourite meat, lamb. Sour flavours (tamarind, turmeric) define the traditional grilled-carp dish *masgouf*.
- **Personal touch worth noting on camera:** spices are so central to Iraqi home cooking that families traditionally have their own unique baharat blend made for them at the local market.
- **Local blend recipe — Arabic Baharat:** grind 1 tbsp black peppercorns, 1 tbsp allspice, 1 tsp cloves, 1 tsp coriander seeds, 1 tsp cumin seeds; combine with 1 tbsp cinnamon and 1 tsp grated nutmeg. An all-purpose rub, marinade, or seasoning.
- **Fact worth citing:** Iran produces over 90% of the world's saffron — the most expensive spice in the world.

## 6. Iran — "Floral, Musky, Sour"

- **Signature spices:** saffron, sumac, rose.
- **Supporting:** dried lime, barberry, angelica, cinnamon, cumin, ginger, turmeric, garlic.
- **Supplementary:** fenugreek leaves, curry powder, paprika, tamarind.
- **Flavour identity:** Iran sits at the historic centre of the Persian Empire and the Silk Road, invaded over centuries by Greeks, Arabs, Turks, Mongols, and Uzbeks — its cuisine reflects that crossroads, featuring caviar, Chinese noodles, smoked fish, and hot-and-sour prawns alongside pistachios, pomegranates, walnuts, and prized homegrown saffron. Stews and pilafs lean on sweet-and-sour spices (sumac, dried limes, barberries, tamarind), while fragrant rose petals bring an exotic note to spice mixes and sweets.
- **Signature detail for camera:** Iran's saffron is used exclusively as whole burnt-red strands — never powder — giving seafood and poultry a rich, slightly bitter flavour and rice its vibrant amber colour.
- **Local blend recipe — Advieh:** grind 2 tbsp dried rose petals, 2 tbsp cardamom seeds, 1 tbsp cumin seeds; combine with 2 tbsp ground cinnamon and 2 tbsp ground ginger. Sprinkled over savoury rice, rubbed on meats, added to stews, or used on Persian rice pudding.

## 7. Egypt — "Fresh, Earthy, Nutty"

- **Signature spices:** cumin, cinnamon.
- **Supporting:** garlic, black pepper, chilli, coriander.
- **Supplementary:** ginger, cloves, bay, cardamom, allspice.
- **Flavour identity:** Egypt's ports were a gateway to Eastern spice riches long before its mercantile peak in the Middle Ages, yet the modern Egyptian palette is notably simpler than other Levantine cuisines. Cumin is the everyday, prevailing flavour, enlivening vegetable soups, meat stews, and grain dishes like koshari. Egyptians tend to keep spicing simple, reaching for baharat when they want more complexity.
- **Local blend recipe — Dukkah:** roast 150g hazelnuts (10 min) and 150g whole almonds (8 min) at 200°C/400°F/Gas 6; dry-fry 1 tbsp coriander seeds, 1 tbsp cumin seeds, and 3 tbsp sesame seeds over high heat 3 minutes; grind everything together with a pinch of salt. An everyday nut-and-spice condiment served with olive oil and bread, or used to season roasted meats and hummus.

## 8. Arabian Peninsula (Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman) — "Rich, Dry, Musky"

- **Signature spices:** baharat, dried lime, bay.
- **Supporting:** saffron, cardamom, clove, cinnamon, cumin, coriander, garlic, chilli, black pepper.
- **Supplementary:** turmeric, nutmeg, caraway.
- **Flavour identity:** shares the Levantine trinity of cumin, coriander, and cinnamon with its northern neighbours, plus dried limes and saffron from Iran. As the peninsula stretches toward Yemen and Oman — with Africa and Asia beyond — the flavours and heat intensify: cloves, black pepper, masala mixes, turmeric, and coconut arrive from Asia, hot chillies from Africa. Omani and Yemeni cuisines are particularly known for their fiery, heady spice blends.
- **Local blend recipe — Hawaij:** grind 1 tbsp black peppercorns, 7 tsp cumin seeds, 1 tbsp cardamom seeds, 1 tbsp coriander seeds; combine with 2 tbsp ground turmeric. A curry-like Yemeni blend used in slow-cooked meats and soups. A sweeter version swaps out the pepper and cumin for ginger, cloves, cinnamon, and fennel, and is stirred into coffee and desserts.
