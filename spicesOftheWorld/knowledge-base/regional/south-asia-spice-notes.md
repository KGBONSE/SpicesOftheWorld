## Regional overview
South Asia's communities each carry their own distinctive seasoning traditions, ranging from a simple sprinkle of cumin to elaborate spice blends. Native spices like cardamom and cinnamon sit at the centre of many local cuisines, while chilli and coriander — both originally foreign imports — are now among the region's most valuable exports.

**Key historic waypoints:**
- **India's Malabar coast** was the main export outlet for Kerala's peppercorns and has long sat at the centre of the global spice trade. In the 16th century, Portugal became the first European nation to enrich itself by trading peppercorns from this coast.
- **Calcutta (Kolkata)** grew out of the colonial trading posts where the overland Silk Road and the maritime Spice Route converged — spice merchants from around the world traded there.
- **Goa** was colonized by the Portuguese in the 16th century, who introduced chillies from the New World — the origin point for chilli's spread across the entire subcontinent.
- **Sri Lanka**, formerly Ceylon, was for centuries the world's only known source of cinnamon; eight cinnamon species are native there, and all but one still grow wild.
- Roughly 75% of India's cardamom is grown in the state of Kerala.
- **Lhasa** (Tibet) was an important stop on the southwest Silk Road.

## 1. North India — "Complex, Earthy, Fragrant"

- **Signature spices:** cumin, turmeric, chilli, ginger, garlic — the foundation of essential masala mixes and pastes.
- **Supporting:** cassia, clove, cardamom, Indian bay, fennel (Indian bay is in the same plant family as cinnamon and tastes like a mix of clove, cinnamon, and allspice).
- **Supplementary:** black cumin, anardana (dried pomegranate seed), peppercorn, saffron.
- **Flavour identity:** spans everything from no-frills Punjabi home cooking to the refined Mughal palace kitchens — kebabs and hearty dhals finished with sizzling cumin at roadside cafés are as internationally travelled as the food itself. During the era of Maharajas and Nawabs, royal chefs built flamboyant spice blends for elaborate dishes, some featuring a dozen or more aromatics; home cooks today favour the warming character of garam masala over raw chilli heat.
- **Local blend recipe — Garam Masala:** grind 25g black cardamom seeds, 25g cassia (broken into pieces), 25g black peppercorns, 10g black cumin seeds, 2 blades of mace, 10g cloves, and ⅛ of a nutmeg (chopped) to a fine powder; sieve out any fibres. Many households have their own variation of this warming blend.

## 2. Himalayan Belt (Nepal, Bhutan, Tibetan regions) — "Astringent, Fiery, Warming"

- **Signature spices:** fenugreek, chilli, timur (a type of Sichuan pepper), ginger, garlic.
- **Supporting:** black cardamom, asafoetida, turmeric.
- **Supplementary:** coriander, ajwain, clove, cumin.
- **Flavour identity:** cooking here is shaped by nature's harvest in the foothills alongside frugal high-altitude staples — an emphasis on wholesome pulses, often finished with a *tarka* of warming fried cumin, chillies, and ginger. In Bhutan, chilli is elevated from seasoning to main ingredient. Ginger-spiced steamed dumplings and chilli-laden Tibetan broths sit alongside Punjabi staples like parathas and naan.
- **Detail worth citing:** black cumin (shahi jeera) comes from a different but related plant to ordinary cumin and carries a slightly smoky flavour. Fenugreek's bitterness is deliberately contrasted against the nuttiness of lentils and pulses; timur brings a citrusy, astringent note to Nepali masalas.
- **Local blend recipe — Timur ko Chhop:** dry-roast 1 tsp timur (or Sichuan pepper) in a dry pan over low heat until fragrant; mix with 2 tbsp red chilli powder and 1 tsp salt; grind to a powder. Described as the Himalayan equivalent of salt and pepper — used on soups, as a dip for fried meats, or as a pick-me-up over fried potatoes, noodles, and curries.

## 3. Central India — "Tart, Nutty, Assertive"

- **Signature spices:** coriander, cumin, dried red chillies, turmeric, ginger.
- **Supporting:** asafoetida, fennel, nigella.
- **Supplementary:** ajwain, amchoor (dried mango powder).
- **Flavour identity:** shaped by neighbouring Rajasthan, Gujarat, and Maharashtra, Central India transforms vegetarian staples into standout dishes through contrasting spice combinations. A well-known simple seasoning here is a trio of fried nigella, fenugreek, and fennel seeds, which gives a pickled edge to softened vegetables and meaty masalas. In Bhopal, cafés serve biryanis, kebabs, and aromatic curries built on garam masala emboldened with plenty of pounded coriander seed.
- **Fact worth citing:** *panch phoran* translates as "five spices" — Bengali versions of the blend substitute local radhuni seed for mustard seed.
- **Local blend recipe — Chaat Masala:** dry-roast 2 tbsp cumin seeds, 1 tsp coriander seeds, and 1 tbsp black peppercorns until fragrant; cool, then mix with 3 tbsp amchoor, 1 tsp ground ginger, ½ tsp asafoetida, 1 tbsp kala namak (Himalayan black salt), and 1 tsp salt; grind to a powder. Sprinkled over street snacks, fruit salads, and kebabs.

## 4. East India and Bangladesh — "Pungent, Sweet-Sour, Mustardy"

- **Signature spices:** chilli, mustard, turmeric, cumin, ginger, garlic.
- **Supporting:** poppy seed, black cardamom, fennel.
- **Supplementary:** coriander, fenugreek, nigella.
- **Flavour identity:** cooks in the river delta of West Bengal and Bangladesh favour complex spice blends that work especially well with fish and seafood. For religious reasons, many Hindus in Kolkata avoid cooking with onion and garlic, leaning instead on nutty-tasting poppy seed paste, popped mustard seeds, ginger, and the aromatic five-spice *panch phoran*. Bangladeshi Muslim cooking favours more assertive seasoning — Mughal-style biryanis and beef curries infused with whole spices.
- **Local blend recipe — Panch Phoran:** simply combine 2 tsp cumin seeds, 2 tsp brown mustard seeds, 2 tsp fennel seeds, 1 tsp nigella seeds, and 1 tsp fenugreek seeds (no grinding required). Used by frying a teaspoonful in oil or ghee at the start of cooking, or tempering a dish just before serving. Particularly good with dhals and vegetable dishes.

## 5. West India (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa) — "Bitter, Sour, Sweet"

- **Signature spices:** kokum, dried red chilli, cumin, turmeric, mustard, ginger, garlic (garlic is shunned by the Jain community for religious reasons).
- **Supporting:** fenugreek, curry leaf, black pepper.
- **Supplementary:** asafoetida, sesame.
- **Flavour identity:** western India's cooking reflects genuinely varied landscapes — Rajasthan's arid climate versus the tropical atmosphere of Maharashtra and Goa. Gujarati savoury dishes balance sweet-sour flavours via jaggery against pungent fenugreek seed and dried red chilli. Royal Rajasthani dishes are known for rich, chilli-laden masalas; along the Konkan coastline, hot-and-sour fish curries are balanced by creamy coconut masalas. Goa is famous for its vinegary, garlicky Indo-Portuguese dishes.
- **Local blend recipe — Vindaloo Paste:** toast 1½ tsp cumin seeds, 15–20 whole dried Kashmiri chillies, 1 tsp black peppercorns, 2 tsp black mustard seeds, 1 tsp fenugreek seeds, 6 cloves, 1 tsp cardamom seeds, a 5cm piece of cassia, and ½ star anise in a dry pan until fragrant; grind, then mix with 6 tbsp cider or palm vinegar, 1 tsp salt, and 2 tsp jaggery (or date palm sugar) into a stiff paste. Goa adopted and adapted this fiery, sweet-sour mix directly from the Portuguese.

## 6. South India and Sri Lanka — "Fresh, Zesty, Robust"

- **Signature spices:** tamarind, mustard, fenugreek, cumin, cardamom, ginger, garlic, curry leaf, chilli.
- **Supporting:** cassia, cinnamon, black pepper, nutmeg, asafoetida.
- **Supplementary:** sesame, star anise, mace.
- **Flavour identity:** from Kerala's cardamom hills to Sri Lanka's cinnamon plantations and Andhra Pradesh's renowned chilli harvest, this region's culinary traditions were shaped by centuries of spice trading — Portuguese, French, Dutch, Southeast Asian, and British influences all adapted to local tastes. The true stalwarts remain sambhar (tart lentils with vegetables), rice in every form, and sour fish masalas. Dishes range from mild and mellow to bold and fiery depending purely on whether the spices are dry-roasted, fried, pounded, or left whole.
- **Fact worth citing:** star anise was most likely introduced to this region by Chinese traders, since very little is actually grown in South Asia — a direct, ready-made link to a future East Asia episode. Kokum, a dried rind from a fruit in the mangosteen family, is used like tamarind to add sourness.
- **Local blend recipe — Gunpowder:** dry-roast 25g chana dal and 25g split urad dal separately until coloured; toast 25g fresh/desiccated coconut; add 1 tbsp black sesame seeds, 2 tbsp curry leaves, and 6–8 red chillies, cooking until the chillies darken; stir in 1 tbsp tamarind pulp and ¼ tsp asafoetida, toast 1 minute, then add 2 tsp jaggery off the heat; combine everything with the lentils, cool, and grind to a coarse powder. Sprinkled over rice, dosa, and idli, or mixed with oil as a dip.
