## Regional overview
The Americas bring together flavours and textures from high mountains, low valleys, seas, oceans, forests, and islands — plus an array of influences from every corner of Asia and Europe. The result is a spice map that's eclectic, bold, and complex.

**Key historic waypoints:**
- **"Two-way traffic":** spices travelled in both directions between continents. Cloves, pepper, and cinnamon reached the New World by sea from Asia; in return, chillies, vanilla, and cocoa beans were transported back to the Old World. This is the trade current that carried chilli — a genuine New World native — into every region you've already covered.
- **"A New World":** between the 15th and 18th centuries, the European search for spices led explorers on voyages into uncharted seas; their discovery of the Americas radically changed the world's food map.
- **Christopher Columbus**, while searching for the source of black pepper, instead stumbled onto allspice in Jamaica in the 1490s — allspice is the only spice grown exclusively in the western hemisphere.
- **Acapulco** was, in the 17th century, where spices arriving from Asia were sent overland to Veracruz and then by sea to Spain — the far end of the same Manila–Acapulco galleon route flagged in your Southeast Asia file.
- **Rio de Janeiro:** in 1502, the first Europeans to see Rio were members of a Portuguese spice expedition.
- The USA is the world's largest importer of spices, with vanilla and pepper the most popular.

## 1. The Caribbean — "Adventurous, Bold, Spicy"

- **Signature spices:** Scotch bonnet chilli, allspice, clove, ginger, nutmeg, cinnamon.
- **Supporting:** black pepper, cumin, garlic, paprika, habanero chilli.
- **Supplementary:** turmeric, coriander, fenugreek, cayenne chilli, nigella.
- **Flavour identity:** when European explorers went looking for the Indies, they found the Caribbean instead. Centuries of empire-building followed, nearly wiping out indigenous peoples and turning the West Indies into a new Spice Route trading hub. That history made Caribbean cuisine the genuine melting pot it is today — fusing Amerindian, African, Creole, Spanish, Dutch, Portuguese, British, Latin American, Persian, South Asian, and Indonesian influences in one place.
- **Detail worth citing:** turmeric, coriander, and fenugreek are integral to West Indian curry powder — a direct South Asian import; roasted nigella seeds flavour breads and vegetable dishes; Mexican oregano (pronounced citrus and aniseed notes) is often added to chilli powder blends.
- **Local blend recipe — Jamaican Jerk Rub:** grind 2 tsp allspice, 1 tsp whole black peppercorns, and ½ tsp cloves; combine with 1 tbsp chipotle or cayenne powder, 1 tsp paprika, 1 tsp grated nutmeg, 1 tsp ground ginger, ½ tsp ground cinnamon, 2 tsp onion powder, 2 tsp garlic powder, 1 tsp dried thyme, 2 tbsp brown sugar, and 2 tsp sea salt. Invented in Jamaica and adapted across the Caribbean to marinate chicken, fish, pork, or beef — flavour intensity depends on marinating time.

## 2. Mexico and Central America — "Hot, Toasted, Complex"

- **Signature spices:** chilli (ancho, jalapeño, pasilla, mulato, chipotle, chile de árbol), paprika, cumin.
- **Supporting:** black pepper, garlic, cinnamon, clove, vanilla.
- **Supplementary:** cacao, sesame, allspice, Mexican oregano, coriander, annatto.
- **Flavour identity:** the range of spices in everyday Mexican and Central American cooking is as complex as the region's layered history. Belizean cuisine draws from the Maya, the Garifuna, Creole, and British influences; Mexican cooking owes debts to Mayan, Aztec, and Spanish-colonial traditions. The Maya frequently used red and green jalapeño chillies; the Aztecs used chipotle chillies, cacao, and vanilla — all of which spread worldwide via the Spanish conquistadores, who in turn brought back cumin, pepper, cloves, and cinnamon.
- **Detail worth citing:** any of 60 different native chillies offer varying heat, used fresh, whole-dried, or powdered; cinnamon traditionally flavours Mexican chocolate bars; annatto is mainly used to colour meats, rice, and stews.
- **Local blend recipe — Mole Mix:** every country has its own version, always built on three different chillies — toast 2 each of dried ancho, pasilla, and mulato chillies with 3 tbsp cacao nibs, 1 tbsp sesame seeds, a 2cm cinnamon stick, ¼ tsp each cumin seeds, anise seeds, whole black peppercorns, and cloves, plus ¼ tsp each dried thyme and marjoram and 1 crumbled dried bay leaf; grind, then stir into fried onions and garlic and simmer with chopped tomatoes to make a mole sauce.

## 3. The Andes (Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, Venezuela) — "Warming, Earthy, Piquant"

- **Signature spices:** chilli (ají amarillo, rocoto/locoto, merquén), cumin, garlic.
- **Supporting:** pepper (white and black), molle (pink pepper), turmeric, cinnamon, paprika (sweet and smoked).
- **Supplementary:** annatto, coriander, mustard.
- **Flavour identity:** spanning some 4,500 miles, the Andes trace a route from Argentina and Chile up through Bolivia, Peru, and Ecuador to Colombia and Venezuela. People living at 1,000–3,000m+ typically slow-cook hearty stews and soups, with chillies playing the dominant role. Ají amarillo (yellow chilli) has been cultivated for more than 8,000 years and was a key ingredient in Inca cuisine.
- **Detail worth citing:** in Andean Peru, *uchucuta* is a spicy salsa made from rocoto chilli and herbs; *llajua* is the Bolivian/Argentine equivalent. Molle are the dried pink berries of the Peruvian peppertree — similar to peppercorns, but from an entirely unrelated plant.
- **Local blend recipe — Chimichurri:** combine 1 tsp chilli powder, ½ tsp dried chilli flakes, ½ tsp paprika, 2 finely chopped garlic cloves, 1 finely chopped onion, 4 tsp finely chopped parsley or coriander, 1 tsp finely chopped oregano, 3 tsp white or red wine vinegar, and 6 tbsp olive oil; season with salt and black pepper. A popular fresh sauce widely served with sausages and steak.

## 4. Amazon Basin (spanning nine countries, principally Brazil, Peru, Colombia) — "Exotic, Mysterious, Hot"

- **Signature spices:** black pepper, chilli (ají amarillo, ojito de pez, pinguita de mono, pucunucho, cumari, malagueta, cayenne), garlic, cumin.
- **Supporting:** annatto, açaí berries, jambú, tonka beans, turmeric.
- **Supplementary:** priprioca, guaraná, ginger, cinnamon, clove, paprika, vanilla, copoazú, macambo.
- **Flavour identity:** the Amazon is the world's largest rainforest, and its cuisine reflects ancestral influence, convergence between its three key countries (Brazil, Peru, Colombia), and European influence — chiefly Portuguese and Spanish. Amazon chillies are some of the world's hottest, hundreds of native species in every shape and size, often used alongside garlic, cumin, and turmeric to marinate meat and fish.
- **Detail worth citing:** jambú is a flower whose numbing effect earns it the nickname "toothache plant," used in *tacacá*, one of Brazil's most popular soups. Guaraná seeds are used more as a stimulant than a flavouring. Copoazú and macambo are both relatives of cacao.
- **Local blend recipe — Tucupí:** blend 2kg peeled sweet cassava root with 750ml water to a thick purée; strain through muslin over a bowl to extract the starchy juice; let settle, then pour off the clear liquid (discarding sediment); cover and leave at room temperature 24 hours to ferment; combine with 2 finely chopped garlic cloves, 3–4 finely chopped hot red chillies, and 1 tsp ground black pepper; bring to the boil and simmer 30 minutes. A Brazilian broth-like sauce served with duck, pork, or fish.

## 5. North America — "Hot, Sweet, Eclectic"

- **Signature spices:** chilli (jalapeño, poblano, ancho, cayenne), garlic, pepper (black and white), vanilla.
- **Supporting:** paprika, mustard, ginger, nutmeg, cinnamon, cloves.
- **Supplementary:** anise, sesame, cumin, paprika, turmeric, mace.
- **Flavour identity:** European settlers brought only a limited range of spices, mainly to preserve food for the long Atlantic crossing. Gradually, spices from Central America (chilli, annatto), the Caribbean (allspice), and South America (molle) left their mark — though only chilli achieved enduring popularity, thanks to Mexico's proximity. Today's North American cuisine is a genuine fusion: Mexican fajitas, Indian curries, and Japanese ramen all sit comfortably in the everyday diet.
- **Detail worth citing:** after garlic and chillies, pepper and vanilla are the most common spice imports, adding everyday warmth to savoury and sweet dishes respectively. Milder mustard forms dominate table condiments; nutmeg, cinnamon, and cloves are the spices of choice for sweet baking.
- **Local blend recipe — BBQ Rub:** mix 1 tbsp ground cumin, ½ tbsp cayenne pepper, 1 tbsp chilli powder, 3 tbsp brown sugar (a Kansas City touch), 1 tbsp ground black pepper, 2 tbsp smoked paprika (from the Memphis rub tradition), 1 tbsp onion powder, 2 tbsp sea salt, and 1 tbsp garlic powder. A dry rub for barbecued meat, from steak and brisket to pulled pork and chicken wings.

## 6. Pacific South America (Panama to Patagonia) — "Fresh, Spicy, Citrus"

- **Signature spices:** chilli (ají limo, panca, chombo), garlic, coriander stalks.
- **Supporting:** cumin, chilli powder, ginger, fresh lime, turmeric.
- **Supplementary:** annatto, sesame, tamarind.
- **Flavour identity:** from the Gulf of Panama to the tip of Patagonia, this vast coastline draws heavily on seafood — fried, grilled, or stewed. Indigenous culture, Spanish colonization, the slave trade, and Asian and Arab migrations have all left their mark, from delicate chilli-and-coconut fish soups to hearty *charquicán* (beef and vegetable stew). Chillies are central to the spice mix, especially fresh in ceviche.
- **Detail worth citing — a direct link back to your East Asia file:** dishes in the Nikkei tradition — a fusion of Japanese and Peruvian cooking — draw on ginger, sesame, tamarind, and umeboshi (pickled plum) paste. Ají limo is a hot chilli with a strong lemony flavour, taking its name from the Lima region.
- **Local blend recipe — Leche de Tigre:** combine ½–1 finely chopped ají limo (or habanero), 1 crushed garlic clove, 2.5cm coarsely grated ginger, 1 tbsp finely chopped fresh coriander stalks, ½ finely chopped small red onion, and the juice of 5 limes; season with salt; refrigerate 1 hour. The marinating liquid for ceviche — traditionally drunk after the fish itself has been eaten.
