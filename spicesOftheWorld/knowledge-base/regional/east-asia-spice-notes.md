## Regional overview
Despite sitting at the far eastern edge of the Old World, East Asia has shaped the global spice trade enormously — from key stops on 15th-century trade routes to being, today, the world's fourth-largest spice-producing region. Most of the spices here are genuinely mild; Sichuan pepper is the tongue-tingling exception.

**Key historic waypoints:**
- **Xi'an** (formerly Chang'an) marked the start of the Silk Road — a 6,400km (4,000-mile) network that was the world's greatest trade thoroughfare until faster sea routes emerged in the 15th century. Its Western Market was a meeting place for traders from across the known world.
- **Kashghar** was the oasis city where the Silk Road's northern and southern routes merged.
- **Turpan** was an important trade centre on the Silk Road's northern branch.
- **Chengdu's market** is known worldwide for the quality of its Sichuan peppercorns.
- **Nagasaki** became the Portuguese traders' Japanese base in the late 16th century.
- **"Flavour exchange"** ran both directions: spices like cassia travelled west into Central Asia and Europe, while ginger and saffron travelled east and became integral to cooking across many regions of China.

## 1. South Korea — "Fiery, Warm, Pungent"

- **Signature spices:** sesame, red chilli, garlic.
- **Supporting:** ginger, black pepper, perilla seeds.
- **Supplementary:** green chilli, cinnamon.
- **Flavour identity:** Korean cooking has only recently gained the global popularity of other long-established Asian cuisines, but its intoxicating blend of heat and acidity — complex, robust, yet subtle — has quickly won fans. Given Korea's location and history of colonization by both China and Japan, influences and spices from these neighbours (chilli, ginger, garlic) play major roles, but it's the techniques of fermentation and preservation that create flavours unique to Korea. The pungency of chilli and garlic is often balanced by the rich, toasty flavour of sesame, or mellowed through fermentation in dishes like kimchi.
- **Local blend recipe — Yangnyeomjang:** dry-roast 2 tsp white sesame seeds until golden; mix with 3 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tbsp toasted sesame oil, ½ tsp rice vinegar, 2 tsp gochugaru (dried chilli flakes), ½ tsp caster sugar or honey, 1 crushed garlic clove, and 1 sliced spring onion. Keeps in the fridge up to a week — a deeply flavoured seasoning sauce, dip, and marinade.

## 2. Japan — "Fresh, Mild, Bright"

- **Signature spices:** sesame, sanshō (similar to Sichuan pepper), ginger.
- **Supporting:** white pepper, chilli, garlic, star anise.
- **Supplementary:** clove, allspice, cinnamon, black pepper.
- **Flavour identity:** for a relatively small archipelago, Japan's cooking and spicing are surprisingly diverse. A southern "barbarian" cuisine developed in the 16th–17th centuries through contact with the few foreigners allowed to visit the island: the Portuguese introduced deep-frying, chillies, and vinegar; the Chinese brought ramen, gyoza, and dumplings; the British introduced curry. This Western-influenced style contrasts with the more subtle *washoku* (traditional Japanese cuisine) found elsewhere in the country.
- **Detail worth citing:** perilla seeds (also called shiso) come from a plant in the mint family, carrying a nutty, slightly menthol-aniseed flavour, and are grown mainly in central Japan.
- **Local blend recipe — Shichimi-togarashi:** simply mix together 2 tbsp finely ground sanshō (or Sichuan pepper), 2 tbsp dried orange, yuzu, or lemon peel, 4 tbsp chilli powder, 2 tbsp nori seaweed flakes, 2 tsp black sesame seeds, 2 tsp hemp/flax/chia seeds, and 2 tsp garlic powder. Used as a table condiment or to flavour curries, grilled meats, and noodle dishes.

## 3. North China (Shandong, Henan, Beijing) — "Salty, Sour, Light"

- **Signature spices:** ginger, sesame, Sichuan pepper.
- **Supporting:** star anise, garlic.
- **Supplementary:** cumin, chilli.
- **Flavour identity:** in a country as vast as China, cuisine varies enormously, but spicing across the north is a more integrated picture, built on the gentle spices shared by Shandong, Henan, and Beijing cooking. Shandong cuisine favours subtle flavours that preserve rather than dominate the main ingredients — and is regarded as the primary cuisine among China's eight major regional traditions. Henan is more delicate but harmonious; Beijing draws from both, with an emphasis on the sour notes of the dark vinegar used throughout the region.
- **Local touch:** cumin and chilli originated in the kitchens of Chinese Muslims — *yangrou chuan* (lamb skewers) coated in cumin and chilli are a Beijing favourite.
- **Local blend recipe — Shandong Spice Bag:** combine 10 cardamom pods, 2 star anise, ½ tbsp whole Sichuan pepper, ½ tbsp cloves, ½ tbsp ground cumin, a 7.5cm cinnamon stick, a 10–12cm piece of liquorice root, and dried orange or tangerine peel in a muslin bag; tie and drop into the cooking water. Used to infuse the master stock for northern Chinese chicken stews (Dezhou- or Daokou-style).

## 4. East China (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Shanghai, Anhui) — "Subtle, Aromatic, Sweet"

- **Signature spices:** ginger, white pepper, Sichuan pepper, sesame.
- **Supporting:** star anise, cinnamon, lotus seed.
- **Supplementary:** black cardamom, fennel, cumin, liquorice.
- **Flavour identity:** eastern Chinese cuisine is a contrasting mix shaped largely by terrain. Inland, the Yellow Mountains and Huangshan area are the preserve of unique foraged ingredients in Anhui cuisine, often sweetened with sugar into hearty peasant food. The coast — Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Shanghai — offers a more refined, gourmet cuisine built on subtly aromatic flavours that highlight the region's abundant seafood and river food.
- **Detail worth citing:** lotus seeds carry a mild, sweet flavour reminiscent of fresh almonds and pine nuts.
- **Local blend recipe — Nanjing Spice Bag:** combine 6 cloves, 4 star anise, 1 tsp crushed whole white pepper, 6 bay leaves, dried tangerine peel, 1 tsp liquorice root powder, and 1 tsp salt in a muslin bag; tie tightly and drop into the cooking water alongside sliced fresh ginger and Shaoxing wine. Used to flavour the stock for renowned Nanjing salted duck.

## 5. South China (Guangdong/Cantonese, Hunan, Fujian, Taiwan) — "Sour, Aromatic, Sweet"

- **Signature spices:** garlic, ginger, chilli, sesame.
- **Supporting:** black pepper, shajiang (sand ginger), five-spice powder.
- **Supplementary:** white pepper, liquorice.
- **Flavour identity:** if any Chinese cuisine has travelled the world, it's Cantonese food from Guangdong — the most southerly of China's eight great food traditions. Extensive emigration from this coastal region carried techniques like steaming and stir-frying worldwide. Simple condiments (light soy sauce, garlic, ginger, sesame oil) dominate Cantonese cooking, while other southern cuisines — Hunan, Fujian, Taiwan — lean on more intense flavours via drying, preserving, and a wider spice palette.
- **Detail worth citing:** shajiang (sand ginger) has the same fresh heat as ginger but with a mildly medicinal edge. In Taiwan, white pepper is often paired with pork belly.
- **Local blend recipe — Five-Spice Powder:** grind 2 star anise, 1 tsp fennel seeds, 1 tsp cloves, a 5cm cinnamon stick, and 1 tbsp whole Sichuan pepper to a fine powder, sieving out fibres if needed. Used as a rub for roasting meats or added to stir-fries and stews — genuinely better homemade than store-bought.

## 6. West China (Sichuan, Xinjiang) — "Fruity, Warm, Earthy"

- **Signature spices:** bird's eye chilli, Sichuan pepper, garlic.
- **Supporting:** star anise, ginger, sesame, black cardamom.
- **Supplementary:** cumin, saffron, cinnamon.
- **Flavour identity:** the Silk Road introduced the Sichuan region to unfamiliar spices carried by travellers from western and central Asia, producing a wide blend of flavour sensations — sour, sweet, bitter, salty, hot, spicy — especially in the far-west Xinjiang region. There, where the population is more than 50% Muslim, spices like cumin and saffron, alongside dried fruits, feature heavily in Uyghur and Kazakh lamb, chicken, and vegetable dishes.
- **Fact worth citing on camera:** western Chinese cuisine has been dominated by Sichuan pepper for more than 2,000 years.
- **Trade note — direct payoff of your Middle East material:** Kashgar's market is described as filled with flavours that made their way to this Uyghur region from the Middle East, specifically cumin, cinnamon, and saffron.
- **Local blend recipe — Chilli Black Bean Sauce:** fry 200g finely chopped red bird's eye chillies and 50g rinsed, chopped fermented black beans in 2 tbsp vegetable oil for 1 minute; add 1 tbsp rice wine and 2 tsp dark rice vinegar, simmer 3 minutes; add 1 tbsp sugar, simmer 10 minutes; add 3 garlic cloves and a 7.5cm piece of chopped ginger, cook until the oil floats to the surface. A homemade version of doubanjiang (Sichuan fermented bean paste) — keeps in the fridge up to 3 weeks.
