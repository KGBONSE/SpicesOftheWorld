Growing up in Ghana, my favourite memories are the trips I took with my mother to one of the busiest, liveliest markets in the country: Mokola Market in Accra. Bursting with the most amazing spices you can imagine, the colours, aromas, sounds, and human energy made it one of the most vibrant, unforgettable places I know — a place that embodies the country's spirit, resilience, and joyful hustle.

There was one stall she always stopped at first — before the fish, before the plantain, before anything else. She'd pick up a handful of small, reddish-brown seeds, crack one open between her fingers, and let me smell it. Sharp, peppery, warm, nothing like the black pepper I knew from school dinners. Those seeds are called grains of paradise, and centuries ago they were traded across the Sahara into Europe as a cheaper stand-in for black pepper — in wine, in beer, all over European kitchens — before the world moved on and West Africa quietly kept them as its own.

That same spice, alongside its smoky cousin grains of selim, is the backbone of Yaji, the dry rub behind two dishes that are really one dish: Ghana's chinchinga and Nigeria's suya. Same mix, same fire, two names, two countries tied together on a plate.

I recently went back to film the real market and shop footage for this story — the stalls, the seasoning shelves, the same colours and textures I grew up around — for the first episode of a project I've been building: a YouTube series that tells global spice history through the lens I grew up with, starting in West Africa.

It's the first of many, and it starts exactly where my own story does.
