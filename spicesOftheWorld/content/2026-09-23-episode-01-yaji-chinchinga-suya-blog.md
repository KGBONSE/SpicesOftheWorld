# Yaji: the one spice mix behind two street foods, two countries, one memory

There was one stall at Mokola Market my mum, God bless her soul, would always stop at first — before the fish, before the plantain, before the koobi, before anything else. She'd pick up a handful of small, reddish-brown seeds, crack one between her fingers, and let me smell it. Sharp, peppery, warm — nothing like the black pepper I knew from school dinners back home. That smell planted the seeds for everything that came after: my love for spices, for farming, for cooking. So today I want to take you back to that exact stall, for the spice Europe once called "grains of paradise" — and for the blend it ends up in, Yaji, the thing that ties Ghana and Nigeria together on a plate.

## Where grains of paradise actually comes from

Grains of paradise doesn't come from anywhere exotic or far-flung — it comes from right here, West Africa, and Ghana is still the world's main producer today. There's a whole stretch of coastline that used to be called the Grain Coast, the Pepper Coast, purely because of how much of this spice once moved through it. Its cousin, grains of selim, travels further still — from Ethiopia all the way to Ghana, and it's especially loved in southern Nigeria too.

## The pepper Europe borrowed, then gave back

Here's the part that gets me every time: hundreds of years ago, Arab, Berber, and Jewish traders carried these grains across the Sahara all the way to Europe, and Europeans gave it that beautiful name — "grains of paradise" — just to sell it as a cheaper stand-in for black pepper. For a while it was everywhere, in wine, in beer, in Europe's kitchens. Then the Portuguese found a direct sea route to Asia, and the world moved on to real black pepper. Grains of paradise and grains of selim quietly went back to being a West African secret. Ours never really left — it just stopped being anyone else's.

## What the spice is actually doing

According to Dr Farrimond, the slow-building warmth in grains of paradise comes down to a compound called paradol — the same one that gives ginger its heat — alongside gingerol for pungency and a bitter, earthy note from humulone. Grains of selim is a different animal altogether: musky, almost smoky, from a compound called fenchone. One cooking tip worth knowing: grains of paradise is gentler than black pepper, so you'll want two to three times as much to get the same punch.

There's real research behind this one too, not just history. Research suggests grains of paradise can help rev up metabolism, even activate the kind of fat tissue that burns calories for heat — interesting context, not a health claim about any product. Grains of selim is one of the better-documented African spices for gut and respiratory wellbeing, and ginger is about the most solidly-backed spice going for digestion. For a street-food rub, Yaji is doing a surprising amount of quiet work.

## The blend: Yaji

This is the mix that ties Ghana and Nigeria together for me. Yaji is grains of selim, dry chillies, crushed kuli-kuli (that's crushed peanut puffs), ginger powder, a crumbled stock cube, salt, and black pepper, all combined into one dry rub. Same mix, whether you call it chinchinga or suya. [Note: the exact ratios below are a working starting point, not yet locked — worth test-cooking before this is called final.]

**Yaji, a starting point:**
- 4 tbsp crushed kuli-kuli (peanut puffs) — the base that carries everything else
- 1 tsp ground grains of selim — potent and musky, start low
- 1–2 tsp cayenne pepper flakes, or a spoon of Fudi People's smoked dry chillies, to taste
- 1 tsp ginger powder
- 1 crumbled stock cube — reduce added salt if the cube is salty
- ½ tsp salt, added after tasting
- ½ tsp black pepper

Mix dry, rub onto a small batch of meat, grill, and taste — adjust the chilli first for heat, then the grains of selim for how musky you want it, then salt last. [VERIFY ALLERGENS AGAINST SUPPLIER — contains peanuts.]

## The dish: chinchinga and suya

Yaji doesn't stop at skewers — the same mix gets rubbed onto kilishi, that dried spiced beef jerky sold in the same markets, and it's just as often dusted straight onto roasted plantain, corn, or boiled eggs as its own street-food seasoning. But the dish it's most famous for is grilled meat skewers coated in this dry rub before they hit the fire — chinchinga, if you grew up in Ghana; suya, if you grew up in Nigeria. Same spice mix, same fire, two names for the same street food. That's the whole story in one bite.

Soak your wooden skewers first, so they don't burn while the meat cooks. Season the meat lightly to start, then once it's on the fire, keep spraying it with water or beer as it cooks to keep it tender — how much depends on how tough the cut is. Once it's close to done, take it off, put it in a bowl, and rub the Yaji mix into it properly, then put it back on the fire just to finish. That crust at the end is the whole point.

## Filmed at the source

I recently went back to shoot real footage for this one — walking through the market stalls where all of this actually starts, plantain and fruit vendors either side, motorbikes weaving through, and into a small provisions shop stacked floor to ceiling with the stock cubes, dried chillies, and seasonings that make up half of what goes into a Yaji rub. It's the same texture and colour I grew up around, and it's finally going into the episode itself rather than staying a memory.

## Grown in Sidcup, rooted in Ghana

That's what stays with me about that stall — about my mum cracking those seeds open in her hand. The rest of the world had grains of paradise and grains of selim for a moment, and let them go. Ghana never let them go. Nigeria never let them go. And now, on a farm in Sidcup, London, I'm growing my own chillies and spices, trying to carry that same handful forward — for the smell, for the memory, for the market, for her.
