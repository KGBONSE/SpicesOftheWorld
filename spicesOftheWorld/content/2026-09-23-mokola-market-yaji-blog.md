# The market that planted the seeds for Fudi People

I still remember the walk into Mokola Market with my mother — the noise of it first, before anything else, then the colour, then the smell of dried pepper and smoked fish and a hundred spice mixes I couldn't yet name. She knew exactly which stall to head for, which trader to trust, which pile of dried chillies actually had the depth she wanted for the week's soup. I was just a boy trailing behind her, but that market planted the seeds for everything I do now.

Years later, on a much smaller patch of ground in Sidcup, on the edge of London, I started growing my own chillies. Then okra. Then the beginnings of the dried spice blends that would become Fudi People. It wasn't a straight line from that market in Accra to a farm in South East London, but looking back, it's the same instinct: taste something real, trace it back to where it came from, and share it with the people around you.

## From Ghana's markets to a London farm

Fudi People is built on three product lines — Spices of Africa, Spices of South Asia, and Spices of East Asia — and the through-line across all three is the same one Mokola Market taught me without me realising it: a spice blend is never just a list of ingredients, it's a place, a history, and usually someone's grandmother's hand measuring by eye rather than by teaspoon.

Everything starts on the farm. The chillies are grown and smoke-dried there, the okra too, and the dried spices are blended and packed out of a home kitchen registered with the local Environmental Health Officer. It's a small operation on purpose, at least for now — I'd rather get the blend right than get it out fast.

## The first blend: Yaji

Yaji is where the Fudi People story properly starts, and it's a good one to talk about because it's not a niche ingredient — it's the spice mix behind suya, the grilled skewered meat sold from roadside stalls across Ghana and Nigeria, and behind chinchinga, its close cousin. Ground peanuts give it body, ginger and chilli give it heat and lift, and a handful of other dried spices round it out into something you'd recognise instantly if you've ever stood at a suya stand watching someone fan the coals.

What I like about Yaji as a starting point is that it's genuinely useful before it's anything else. It's not a garnish. Rub it onto meat, vegetables, or tofu before grilling or pan-searing, and it does the work of a marinade and a finishing spice at once. That's the standard I hold every blend to — if I wouldn't reach for it myself, on a weeknight, it doesn't go in a jar.

## One dish, done properly: suya at home

You don't need a grill built from an oil drum to make suya — though if you've ever had it cooked over one at a roadside stall in Lagos or Accra, you'll know that's still the benchmark. At home, a hot cast-iron pan or a grill pan gets you most of the way there.

Coat thin strips of beef, chicken, or firm vegetables generously in Yaji, let it sit for at least twenty minutes so the ground peanut and spice mix can cling and start to work into the meat, then cook it hot and fast until the outside catches slightly and turns a little dark at the edges — that's the flavour, not a mistake. A squeeze of lime and a few raw onion slices on the side is traditional, and it cuts through the richness of the peanut perfectly.

It's the same principle behind a couple of Yaji's regional cousins worth knowing: tsire, which is essentially suya by another name across much of Northern Nigeria, and the version served with extra chilli and onion as a street-food snack rather than a main. Different names, same idea — a market-taught spice mix doing more work than people expect.

## Why I'm telling this story on camera

I'm building out a YouTube channel that tells spice history through the lens I grew up with — an African one first, since that's where my own story starts, then working outward. Every episode traces a blend back to its region, its history, and the dish it actually belongs in, the same way my mother could have told you exactly where a pile of dried pepper at Mokola Market had come from and what it was for.
